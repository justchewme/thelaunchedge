The problem it solves.

Most leadership teams don't have a strategy problem. They have a follow-through problem. Decisions get made in a meeting and then dissolve into chat threads. Nobody is quite sure who owns what. The CEO spends the week reading channels and asking "where are we on this?"

An operating cadence takes the chasing off the leader's plate. Every item has an owner and a date, the week has a fixed rhythm, and what reaches the CEO is only what needs a decision, with a recommendation attached.

The leader decides. The system makes sure the right things reach the leader, and that the decision gets done.

How the six weeks run.

  1. Map. We sit with the leadership team and map who owns what, where decisions stall and what the CEO spends the week chasing. You get a one-page map and a build plan.
  2. Design. We agree the weekly rhythm: what is reviewed, by whom, when, and what counts as a decision that needs the CEO. We write down the rules.
  3. Run. We run the cadence live for a few weeks alongside the team, fixing what doesn't work as we go.
  4. Tune. We tighten what gets surfaced and what gets ignored, until the leadership team's week runs on it.
  5. Hand over. Your team gets the setup, the rules and a walkthrough, and the people who will run it are trained. I step out.

What you get.

  • i.
    An ownership map. Who owns which area and which recurring decisions, written down so everyone reads from the same page.
  • ii.
    A weekly rhythm. What's open, what's late, what's waiting on the CEO, and what changed. Short enough to read before the first meeting.
  • iii.
    Decisions with a recommendation. Each one framed so the CEO can say yes, change it or discuss it, in minutes.
  • iv.
    A decision record. Every decision, owner and deadline in one place, so nothing depends on someone's memory.
  • v.
    A trained owner on your team. Someone who can adjust the rhythm without calling me.
