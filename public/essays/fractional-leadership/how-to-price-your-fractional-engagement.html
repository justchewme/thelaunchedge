How to price your fractional engagement.

Most fractional operators underprice themselves by making the same mistake: they take their last annual salary, divide it by working hours, and treat the result as a rate. That is a labour calculation. Fractional pricing is not a labour calculation — it is a value calculation, and conflating the two costs you money every engagement for as long as you stay in the model.

The salary anchor problem.

Here is how the math typically goes wrong. An operator who was earning SGD 180,000 a year divides that by 48 working weeks, then by five days a week, then decides that charging SGD 750 a day seems reasonable — maybe even generous, because they are adding a "freelance premium." They propose this to a founder, the founder accepts without blinking, and the operator spends three months doing excellent work before realising they are generating less than they made as an employee, with no benefits, no job security, and a pipeline they still need to build.

The salary anchor is seductive because it feels rigorous. It has numbers in it. But it is measuring the wrong thing. Your salary was a compensation for your time and labour over a year. Your fractional rate is compensation for the outcome you are delivering to a specific company at a specific moment in their trajectory. Those are different products. A company that is trying to raise its Series B in six months and needs someone to clean up its financial reporting and build its investor narrative is not buying forty hours of your month. It is buying the outcome of a successful raise — and the value of that outcome vastly exceeds any hourly calculation you could construct.

The operators who price well understand this distinction intuitively. They ask a different opening question. Not "what am I worth per hour?" but "what is the problem this company is trying to solve, and what does solving it actually mean for them?"

Why day rates compress your value.

Even when an operator escapes the salary anchor, many still default to day rates as the pricing mechanism. Day rates are common, familiar, and easy to compare — which is precisely why they are structurally bad for a fractional operator.

When you price by the day, you make your time the unit of value. The client starts thinking in terms of how many days they are getting and whether those days were "worth it." You start thinking about how to justify the time you spent rather than whether you are solving the right problems. Both of you are now optimising for the wrong thing.

Day rates also make your pricing visible in a way that invites comparison to entirely the wrong benchmarks. A founder who is quoted SGD 1,800 per day for a fractional COO will immediately start comparing that to other consultants, to a junior hire's daily cost, or to what they paid a vendor last quarter. None of those comparisons are meaningful. You are not a vendor, you are not a junior hire, and you are not doing what other consultants do. But the day rate format invites those comparisons because it looks like a commodity price on a commodity unit.

The alternative is to price on scope and outcome. Define what you are accountable for — the decisions you will own, the functions you will oversee, the deliverables you will produce — and quote a monthly retainer against that scope. The conversation shifts from "how many days do we get?" to "what are we trying to achieve?" That is a much better conversation to be having.

What the Singapore market actually looks like.

For operators who want concrete numbers rather than frameworks: here is what the Singapore fractional market actually produces at each tier, based on engagements I have seen close and structured directly.

Entry-level fractional operators — typically former mid-to-senior managers with eight to twelve years of experience, no prior fractional track record — generally land their first engagements in the SGD 5,000 to SGD 8,000 per month range for two days a week. This is not the right price for someone with genuine senior operator experience; it is the discount that comes from not yet having the proof points and the confidence to charge more. Most operators in this range move up within two to three engagements as they accumulate case studies.

Mid-tier fractional operators — those with at least one prior fractional engagement and a demonstrable outcome they can point to — typically price in the SGD 8,000 to SGD 13,000 per month range for two days a week. This is the largest segment of the market in Singapore and where most of the serious work gets done. Founders in Series A and Series B companies who have made their first fractional hire are usually in this range.

Senior fractional operators — those with C-suite track records, sector-specific expertise that is in short supply, or a strong referral network that reduces the client's perceived risk — price from SGD 13,000 to SGD 20,000 per month. This is not a large segment by volume, but it is where the most interesting work happens. Operators at this level are often running two or three of these engagements simultaneously, which produces an income that materially exceeds what most full-time C-suite roles would pay.

One practical observation: in Singapore specifically, founders at funded startups tend to accept retainer pricing more readily than day rates, partly because they are accustomed to SaaS subscription pricing in their own businesses. Founders of SMEs and family businesses are more likely to want day rates initially. If you are working with the latter group, it is worth taking the time to explain why the retainer structure benefits them — it keeps your focus on their outcomes rather than your utilisation.

Your salary was compensation for your time. Your fractional rate is compensation for an outcome. Pricing the same way in both contexts is the core mistake.

How to anchor your price on value, not labour.

Before you quote any number to a prospective client, you need to answer three questions about the engagement.

First: what is the specific outcome the company is trying to achieve in the next six to twelve months? Not "improve operations" — the actual outcome. Close a Series B. Hit SGD 10M ARR. Bring the supply chain in-house. Reduce headcount cost by 20 percent without losing output quality. The more specific the outcome, the more clearly you can connect your work to its value.

Second: what is that outcome worth to the company? Not to you — to them. A successful Series B raise might bring in SGD 8M. Reducing the headcount cost by 20 percent might save SGD 600,000 a year. Your engagement fee, framed against those numbers, will look very different than it looks against an hourly rate calculation. This is not manipulation — it is helping the founder understand what they are actually buying.

Third: what is the cost to the company of not solving this problem? The alternative to hiring a fractional operator is not zero — it is the founder continuing to manage the function badly, or hiring someone full-time who is not quite right, or letting the problem compound for another twelve months. Understanding the cost of inaction is part of making the value case for your fee.

When you can answer all three questions clearly, the pricing conversation becomes straightforward. You are not defending a rate — you are explaining an investment thesis.

Scope creep and how to charge for it.

One of the most common pricing failures in fractional engagements is not the initial rate — it is the failure to manage scope once the engagement is underway. A founder who has a fractional COO available for two days a week will, within three months, have found seventeen other things they would like that person to do. Some of those things are within scope. Many are not. The operator who says yes to everything out of a desire to be helpful ends up providing four days of value for two days of pay and resenting the engagement.

The solution is a clear scope definition at the outset — written, not verbal — and a standing mechanism for discussing scope additions. When a founder asks for something outside the scope, the right response is not "no" and not "yes." It is "that is outside the current scope; I can add it for an additional SGD X per month or we can discuss what to trade off to keep the fee the same." This keeps the relationship honest, keeps your economics intact, and — importantly — trains the founder to think clearly about what they are actually asking for.

Operators who handle this well charge more over time, not less. Each scope conversation is an opportunity to demonstrate that you understand the business well enough to know what is in scope and what is not, and that you have the discipline to manage the engagement rather than just react to whatever the founder needs on any given day.

  • 01
    Price on scope, not time. Define what you are accountable for — the decisions you own, the function you oversee, the outcome you are targeting — and quote a monthly retainer against that scope. Never let the conversation default to days or hours.
  • 02
    Anchor to the outcome's value, not your labour cost. Before quoting, calculate what the target outcome is worth to the company and what inaction costs them. Your fee should be a fraction of the value delivered, not a function of the hours you spend.
  • 03
    Know the Singapore market ranges and price at the right tier. Entry-level fractionals: SGD 5,000–8,000/month. Mid-tier: SGD 8,000–13,000/month. Senior: SGD 13,000–20,000/month. Price below your tier and you signal lack of confidence. Price above it without the proof points and you lose deals you should win.
  • 04
    Define scope in writing before the engagement starts. What is included, what is not, and what the mechanism is for adding to scope. This protects you from scope creep and protects the founder from ambiguity about what they are paying for.
  • 05
    Never discount the rate; adjust the scope instead. If a company cannot afford your full rate, offer a reduced scope for the same rate — fewer days, a narrower mandate, a shorter initial commitment. Discounting the rate tells the market that your rate was arbitrary. Reducing the scope tells them that your rate reflects real value and they are getting what they pay for.
  • 06
    Review and raise rates at every contract renewal. If you are delivering real value, a 15–20 percent increase at renewal is defensible. Operators who do not raise rates are implicitly agreeing that the work has not increased in value — which is almost never true in a well-run fractional engagement.

Frequently asked questions.

What is a reasonable monthly retainer for a fractional COO in Singapore?

For a Series A company getting two days a week of genuine senior operator time, the range in Singapore runs from SGD 8,000 to SGD 15,000 per month depending on the operator's track record and the complexity of the engagement. Below SGD 7,000 and you are almost certainly underpriced for someone with the credentials to justify the title. Above SGD 18,000 and you are in territory where founders start comparing you to a full-time hire. The right number is where the client can see the value clearly and you are not resentful of the work. Both conditions have to hold.

Should I charge a day rate or a monthly retainer?

Monthly retainer, almost always. Day rates create the wrong incentive structure for both parties. The client optimises to use fewer days. You optimise to look busy. Neither of you is focused on outcomes. A monthly retainer defines a scope of engagement — what you are accountable for, what decisions you own, what you will deliver — and prices that accountability rather than your calendar time. It also gives you predictable revenue, which matters enormously when you are running multiple clients simultaneously.

How do I justify raising my rates mid-engagement?

By documenting outcomes before the conversation, not during it. If you have been tracking the decisions you made, the problems you solved, and the business impact of those interventions, the rate increase conversation is a review of results, not a negotiation about money. Operators who raise rates cleanly tend to do it at a natural contract renewal point, with three to four weeks of notice, and with a short summary of what changed in the business since the engagement started. Founders who are getting real value rarely push back on a 15 to 20 percent increase at renewal.

What happens if a client says my rate is too high?

Do not discount the rate. If anything, a founder who immediately objects to your rate without having heard what the engagement would cover has not understood what they are buying. Walk them through the scope first — what you would own, what decisions you would make, what outcomes you are targeting — and then quote the number. If the number is still too high, the honest answer is usually one of three things: the company cannot afford senior fractional help right now, you have not made the value case clearly enough, or they do not actually need the level of seniority you bring. All three are worth knowing before you take the engagement.
