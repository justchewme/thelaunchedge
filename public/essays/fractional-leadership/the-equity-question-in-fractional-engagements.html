The equity question in fractional engagements

Equity is the most awkward conversation in fractional work. Clients offer it as a substitute for cash they do not want to spend, or as a loyalty signal they think will make you more committed. Fractional operators demand it as proof the client is serious, or accept it because they are excited about the company and do not want to seem mercenary. Most of these conversations end in structures that do not serve either party well. This essay covers when equity in a fractional engagement makes sense, what it should look like, and the questions that tell you whether the conversation is going well or sideways.

Why equity comes up.

The equity conversation in fractional work usually happens for one of four reasons, and knowing which one you are in shapes how you should respond.

The first reason is cash constraint. The company wants senior talent it cannot fully afford at market rates and is hoping equity makes up the gap. This is the most common reason at pre-seed and seed stage, and it is not inherently bad — early-stage companies are supposed to use equity to access talent they cannot yet afford in cash. The question is whether the equity offered is priced correctly for the risk and the discount you are taking on cash.

The second reason is commitment signalling. The founder believes that giving you equity makes you a stakeholder and therefore more motivated and invested in the outcome. This is a reasonable intuition at full-time employment levels. At fractional engagement levels, where you are likely working with more than one client at a time, it is less coherent — the equity does not materially change your incentive structure relative to your professional reputation and the current cash.

The third reason is structure anxiety. The founder is not sure what a fractional engagement looks like and defaults to the employment model they know, which includes equity. This is well-intentioned but often leads to equity offers structured like employee grants — four-year vests with one-year cliffs — applied to engagements expected to last twelve months.

The fourth reason is genuine alignment. The company has a specific strategic initiative with a defined outcome and a defined time horizon, believes you are central to that outcome, and wants you to share in the upside if the initiative succeeds. This is the only reason that produces a consistently good outcome, and it is the least common.

When equity makes sense.

Equity in a fractional engagement makes sense when three conditions are simultaneously true. The company's cash-to-equity ratio is genuinely constrained relative to the market rate for your engagement — not just because they prefer to pay in equity, but because paying market rate in cash would create a financial problem for the company. The equity offered is priced to reflect the discount you are taking — a meaningful reduction in cash rate translates to a meaningful equity grant, not a token one. And the engagement has a duration and scope that makes the equity outcome plausible — the company is at a stage where a genuine liquidity event in a reasonable time horizon is possible.

When these three conditions are met, equity in a fractional engagement is a legitimate instrument. The operator takes concentrated risk in exchange for upside. The company conserves cash while accessing senior talent. The alignment is genuine because both parties have put real stakes on the table.

When only one or two of the conditions are met, equity is usually a bad deal for the operator. A company that could afford full cash rates but prefers to pay in equity is asking you to bear risk they could absorb themselves. A company offering a token equity grant alongside market-rate cash is making a gesture, not a real offer. A company whose equity is so early-stage and so uncertain that a plausible exit in ten years is the best case is asking you to be unpaid for the present in exchange for a lottery ticket.

A four-year vest with a one-year cliff on a twelve-month fractional engagement is not a compensation structure. It is an employment template applied to a consulting relationship by someone who did not think it through.

How to value it.

The valuation framework for equity in a fractional context is simple and consistently applied too optimistically by both sides. The expected value of an equity stake is: (probability of meaningful exit) × (your percentage of exit proceeds). Both inputs need to be honest to produce a useful number.

Exit probability for early-stage startups is low. Most venture-backed startups never reach a Series B. The share that reach a liquidity event producing meaningful returns to common shareholders is lower still, particularly once you account for preference stacks. For pre-seed and seed-stage companies, assume a low probability that common shareholders see meaningful proceeds — the example below uses 20%, which is a generous assumption for most companies.

Run the arithmetic. A 0.5% stake in a company currently valued at $5M that could exit at $50M in five years, with a 20% probability of a meaningful common equity outcome, produces an expected value of approximately $50K. Compare that to the cash discount you are taking over twelve months to receive that equity. If the discount is $120K, the equity is not compensating you adequately for the risk. If the discount is $30K, it might be.

This arithmetic makes most equity-as-cash-substitute offers look unattractive. The ones that look attractive are typically at companies that have already de-risked significantly — a Series A company with product-market fit and a clear path to Series B — where the exit probability is meaningfully higher and the equity is supplementing, rather than replacing, a real cash rate.

Structures that work.

The equity structures that work in fractional engagements are simpler than employee equity structures and built around the actual duration and nature of the engagement.

At seed stage, where cash constraint is real and equity is taking the place of a meaningful cash discount, the right structure is a grant with a vest period matched to the engagement length. For a twelve-month engagement, a twelve to eighteen month vest with no cliff, or a three to six month cliff, is appropriate. Four years is not. The milestone trigger — equity vesting accelerated by a specific outcome — is also useful in fractional contexts where the operator is engaged specifically to achieve a defined goal.

At Series A and beyond, where the company has raised capital and cash conservation is less the issue, equity in a fractional engagement should be structured as an advisor grant rather than an executive grant. As a rough rule of thumb, advisor grants at Series A sit in the region of 0.1% to 0.25%, often with a two-year vest. These are supplemental to a real cash rate, not substitutes for it. They are appropriate when the operator is providing genuine strategic value and the company wants to create alignment — not when the company is trying to underpay and make up the difference in paper equity.

The worst structure, and one that comes up more often than it should, is the equity-only fractional engagement with no cash and a large grant designed to look like a co-founder allocation. This is not a fractional engagement — it is a co-founder relationship with fractional commitment, which is a different and significantly more complex arrangement. If a company wants co-founder-level equity and alignment, they should hire a co-founder. If they want a fractional executive, they should pay for one.

The conversation you need to have.

The equity conversation in fractional work is more useful when it is explicit about the underlying structure. Before agreeing to any equity component, both parties should be able to answer: What is the current valuation and cap table structure? What is the liquidation preference stack above common? What is the expected timeline to a liquidity event, and what is the realistic exit scenario range? What cash discount is being taken to receive this equity, and what is the expected value of the equity relative to that discount?

Founders who cannot answer these questions have not thought carefully enough about the offer they are making. Operators who do not ask them are accepting risk they have not quantified. The awkwardness of the equity conversation in fractional work comes from both parties avoiding the arithmetic. The arithmetic is not that complicated. It just produces numbers that are often inconvenient for the founder's preferred narrative about the equity's value.

  • 01
    Match the vest period to the engagement length. A twelve-month fractional engagement should have a twelve to eighteen month vest, not a four-year one. Employee equity templates applied to fractional engagements are a structural mismatch that benefits nobody.
  • 02
    Run the expected value arithmetic before agreeing. Probability of exit × your percentage × exit proceeds = expected value. Compare that to the cash discount you are taking. If the arithmetic does not work, the equity is not real compensation.
  • 03
    Equity-only fractional engagements are co-founder structures in disguise. If there is no cash component at all, the relationship is not fractional — it is a co-founder arrangement with ambiguous commitment. That requires a different conversation and a different structure.
  • 04
    At Series A and beyond, equity should be advisor-grant scale. A fully funded Series A company offering a fractional executive large equity grants in lieu of competitive cash is asking you to subsidise their payroll. Advisor grants (0.1–0.25%) supplementing real cash rates are the appropriate instrument.
  • 05
    Ask about the liquidation preference stack before valuing equity. A 1.5× non-participating preferred in a single series is manageable. Multiple liquidation preferences across multiple rounds can make common equity worth very little even in an apparent exit. Know the cap table structure before pricing the stake.

Frequently asked questions.

What is a reasonable equity range for a fractional executive at seed stage?

For a fractional engagement at 2–3 days per week over 12 months at a seed-stage company with a pre-money valuation under $5M, a range of 0.25% to 0.75% is defensible if equity is being accepted instead of a meaningful portion of cash. If the engagement is fully cash-compensated, a smaller advisor-range grant of 0.1% to 0.25% is more appropriate. These numbers compress significantly at Series A, where the company has more capital and the fractional contribution is typically narrower.

Should equity in a fractional engagement vest on the same schedule as employee equity?

Not usually. A standard 4-year vest with 1-year cliff makes sense for a full-time employee. For a fractional engagement expected to last 12–18 months, a 2-year vest with no cliff or a 6-month cliff is more appropriate. The vesting schedule should match the expected duration of the engagement, not default to the employee template.

Is it a red flag if a client offers equity but no cash?

Yes, in most cases. Equity-only compensation for a fractional engagement signals one of three things: the company cannot afford cash (a cash flow problem), the company does not value the engagement enough to pay for it (a respect problem), or the founders have an unrealistic view of their equity's current value (a judgment problem). Any of these warrants scrutiny. Accepting equity-only means you are taking concentrated risk with no liquidity and no guarantee of return.

How do you value equity versus cash in a fractional negotiation?

Use a simple expected value framework: estimated exit probability × estimated exit multiple × your percentage = expected cash value. Be honest about both inputs. For early-stage companies, exit probability is low and exit timelines are long. A 0.5% stake in a company valued at $3M today that might exit at $30M in 6 years, with a 20% exit probability, is worth roughly $30K in expected value — likely worth less than the cash discount you would be giving up to take it.
