Essay · fractional leadership

When fractional is the wrong answer.

There is a version of the fractional pitch that makes it sound like the solution to every problem: you get senior leadership at a fraction of the cost, with flexibility built in. That pitch is true in the right conditions. In the wrong conditions, a fractional engagement wastes everyone's time, damages the company, and ends with both parties wondering what went wrong. The honest diagnostic is worth doing before you sign anything.

The three situations where fractional reliably fails.

Not every company is a good candidate for fractional leadership, and the companies that are not good candidates tend to fall into recognisable patterns. Understanding these patterns before you engage is the operator's job — and the founder's job. A fractional operator who takes an engagement they know is structurally wrong is not being helpful. They are being well-paid for a short period while the company's problems compound underneath them.

The three situations that reliably produce failed fractional engagements are: companies with a culture of deference where the founder cannot genuinely delegate, companies in a genuine operational crisis that requires full-time attention, and companies where the underlying problem is junior capacity rather than senior judgment. Each of these is worth understanding in detail, because each presents differently in the pre-engagement conversation and each produces a different kind of failure.

The deference problem.

A culture of deference is one where all meaningful decisions flow upward to the founder or CEO regardless of who nominally owns an area. The operations lead recommends a vendor but waits for CEO sign-off before proceeding. The finance manager prepares a budget but the founder changes every number in the review meeting. The head of people has a hiring process but the founder interviews every final candidate and overrides the team's recommendation half the time. Everyone has a title. No one has real authority.

This is one of the most common patterns in founder-led companies at the Series A and B stage, and it is one of the most destructive. The founder who built the company by making every decision fast has not learned to trust anyone else's judgment, even as the organisation has grown past the point where one person can make every good decision at the required speed. The leadership team has adapted to this dynamic by not making decisions — by presenting options and waiting for the founder to choose, rather than exercising their own judgment and owning the outcome.

A fractional operator stepping into this environment will encounter the same dynamic immediately. They will have a title — fractional COO, fractional CFO, fractional CPO — but the actual decision-making authority will stay with the founder. The fractional will propose. The founder will approve, modify, or veto. The fractional will implement what the founder chose rather than what their own judgment indicated. This is not fractional leadership. This is a well-compensated implementation layer that happens to cost more than a junior hire.

The diagnostic in the pre-engagement conversation is direct: ask the founder to give you a specific example of a decision that a senior leader in the function you would be taking over made in the last ninety days without requiring CEO sign-off. If they cannot give you one — or if the example they give is a trivial operational decision rather than a meaningful strategic or resourcing choice — the culture of deference is real. A fractional engagement will not fix this. The founder has to change their behaviour first, and that is a different problem from what fractional leadership solves.

The crisis problem.

A company in genuine operational crisis — cash running out, a major customer departure, a leadership team collapse, a regulatory enforcement action — needs something different from what the fractional model provides. It needs someone in the building, every day, who can respond to the situation as it develops in real time, who can be physically present with the team, who can make decisions in the afternoon based on information that arrived in the morning.

The fractional model is designed for a different operating environment: a company that is broadly functional and needs senior leadership to lift a specific function. The fractional operator's value comes from focused, high-quality presence on their assigned days. That value depends on the company being stable enough that the fractional can focus on what they were hired for, rather than plugging gaps across multiple functions or responding to daily emergencies.

A company in crisis does not give the fractional operator that stability. Every engagement day is spent on whatever has exploded since the last time the operator was in. The planned work never gets done. The company does not get the focused senior judgment it is paying for. The operator feels like they are failing because they cannot make progress on anything. In reality, the situation was not appropriate for a fractional engagement in the first place.

What a company in crisis needs is a full-time interim executive — someone whose entire professional focus is on the crisis response for the duration of the emergency. Interim CEOs, interim CFOs, and interim COOs are a different product from fractional operators. They work full-time, usually at a compressed salary or an equity arrangement, for a defined period. They are designed exactly for this situation. Fractional operators are not.

The capacity problem.

The third pattern is subtler and more common than the first two. A company has a functional area — operations, finance, product, marketing — that is not performing. The founder concludes that what is needed is more senior leadership in that area. They hire a fractional operator to provide it. What they discover six months later is that the function improved somewhat but not dramatically, and the fractional engagement consumed significant budget without resolving the underlying issue.

The reason, in most cases, is that the problem was not a lack of senior judgment. It was a lack of junior capacity. The operations function had one person doing the work of three. Adding a fractional COO did not add any operational capacity — it added one senior person who, on their assigned days, could identify what needed to be done but had no one to do it. The function remained under-resourced. The fractional identified the right problems and could not solve them because the team was too thin to execute.

The diagnostic is to ask: if the function had the right senior judgment applied to it tomorrow, would it be able to execute on that judgment? If the answer is no — if the team is too small, too junior, or too stretched to implement decisions even when they are well-made — then the investment priority is additional headcount, not fractional leadership. A fractional operator in an under-resourced team will produce excellent diagnostics and excellent recommendations that the team has no capacity to implement. This is a better outcome than doing nothing, but it is not a fractional leadership success story.

A fractional engagement is not a substitute for giving someone real authority. If the founder cannot genuinely delegate decisions, the fractional model will not work — regardless of how good the operator is.

What the right conditions actually look like.

Describing the failure modes is only useful if it is paired with a description of what the right conditions look like. Fractional leadership works well in a specific and identifiable set of situations.

It works well when a company has a functional gap at the senior level that it cannot yet justify filling full-time. A Series A company that needs a CFO two days a week while it builds toward the revenue level that justifies a full-time hire is the canonical fractional use case. The function needs senior judgment. The company does not yet need that judgment full-time. The fractional model closes the gap.

It works well when the company's leadership team can genuinely delegate. If the founder has demonstrated — not just claimed — the ability to let a senior leader own a function and make decisions without constant escalation, a fractional operator can function at the level their title implies. They will make calls, own outcomes, and drive the function. The engagement will produce results.

It works well when the problem is bounded and the team has the capacity to execute. If the function needs a specific set of improvements — a new finance operating cadence, a product development process, a hiring infrastructure — and the team has the people to implement those improvements once the design is done, a fractional engagement is an efficient way to get there. The fractional provides the senior design and oversight. The team provides the execution capacity. Both sides bring what they are good at.

  • 01
    Test real delegation before committing. Ask the founder for a concrete example of a senior decision made without CEO sign-off in the last ninety days. A culture of deference is the single most common reason fractional engagements underperform. If the founder cannot give you a specific example, the problem is governance, not capability.
  • 02
    Do not take crisis engagements as a fractional. If the company is in genuine operational crisis — cash crisis, leadership collapse, regulatory action — it needs a full-time interim, not a fractional. Recommend the right model even if it costs you the engagement. The alternative is taking a fee for work that cannot produce the outcome the company needs.
  • 03
    Distinguish between a senior judgment gap and a capacity gap. If the function cannot execute decisions even when they are made well, adding a fractional operator will not solve the problem. The investment priority is headcount, not senior oversight. Name this clearly in the diagnostic conversation rather than taking the engagement and discovering it six months in.
  • 04
    Be honest about what two days a week can and cannot do. A fractional operator on two days a week can own a well-defined function in a stable company. They cannot be the primary responder to daily operational fires, cannot manage a team that requires constant supervision, and cannot drive execution in a company that needs someone physically present every day.
  • 05
    Write the scope with the failure conditions in mind. The engagement scope should specify not just what the fractional will do but what the company will do — including who will own execution on non-fractional days, who has authority to make decisions when the fractional is not present, and what the escalation path is for situations outside the scope. Ambiguity on these points is where most engagements go wrong.

Frequently asked questions.

How do I know if my company is ready for a fractional engagement?

Three conditions need to be true. First, the problem requires senior judgment, not more junior capacity — if what you need is more hands doing work, a fractional operator is not the solution. Second, the founder and leadership team are genuinely willing to delegate decision-making authority in the fractional's scope — if you will second-guess every decision or require sign-off on everything, the engagement will not function. Third, the company is stable enough operationally that the fractional can focus on the problem they were hired for, rather than plugging gaps across multiple functions. If any of these three conditions is absent, the engagement will underperform regardless of how good the operator is.

What should a company in crisis hire instead of a fractional operator?

A full-time interim executive, at minimum. Most genuine company crises — cash running out, leadership departures, a failed fundraise, a regulatory problem — require someone in the building who can be physically present, who can attend every meeting, who can make rapid decisions with the latest information, and who the team can see and access throughout the day. A fractional operator who is present two days a week cannot provide that. The interim model is designed for exactly this situation: a full-time senior operator, often at a compressed salary or equity arrangement, who owns the crisis response for the duration of the emergency.

Can a fractional engagement be converted to a full-time hire?

Yes, and this is one of the better outcomes when the fit is strong. A fractional engagement is often the lowest-risk way to evaluate whether a particular operator is the right permanent hire. Both parties have seen each other operate under real conditions. The operator understands the company's actual state rather than the polished version presented in an interview. If the answer is yes and the company's needs have grown to require full-time attention, the conversion conversation is usually straightforward. The fractional rate does not translate directly to salary — compensation gets restructured — but the trust built during the engagement makes the negotiation simpler.

What does a culture of deference look like, and why does it block fractional work?

A culture of deference is one where decisions consistently get escalated to the founder or CEO regardless of who nominally owns the area. The CFO recommends a vendor but waits for the CEO to approve before proceeding. The ops lead identifies a process change but needs the founder's sign-off to implement it. Everyone has a title but no one has real authority. In this environment, a fractional operator has the same experience as every other leader in the company: they can diagnose, recommend, and propose, but they cannot actually move anything without CEO approval on each step. This is not a fractional problem — it is a company culture problem — and a fractional engagement will not solve it.
