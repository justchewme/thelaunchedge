Building a fractional practice from scratch.

Most operators who make the move to fractional work treat it like a career decision. It is not. It is a business model decision — and the skills that made you an excellent operator inside a company are only a subset of what you need to build a practice that generates reliable income across multiple clients over time.

The category error that most people make.

When experienced operators decide to go fractional, the framing they usually apply is something like this: "I have fifteen years of operational experience. Companies need that experience. I will offer my time to multiple companies instead of one." This is correct as far as it goes. It is also incomplete in a way that causes significant pain in year one.

The problem is that the transition from full-time operator to fractional practice is not a lifestyle change — it is a business model change. As a full-time operator, your employer handles pipeline, pricing, brand, and the operational infrastructure of the employment relationship. Your job is to produce great work in your function. As a fractional, all of those things become your job simultaneously with the work itself. You are no longer just an operator. You are an operator who is also running a small professional services business, with all of the attendant demands on your attention and time.

The operators who underestimate this are the ones who get the first client — usually from their network, usually at a below-market rate, usually on terms that were negotiated informally — and spend the next six months doing good work for one client while their pipeline dries up. When that engagement ends, they are back to zero, running the same search they ran eight months earlier, having learned nothing about how to build a sustainable book of business.

The operators who get this right treat the practice-building problem with the same rigor they bring to operational problems. They diagnose the bottleneck, they build the right infrastructure for the stage they are at, and they iterate based on feedback. The inputs are different from what they are used to. The discipline is the same.

Positioning: the thing you have to get specific about.

The single most important decision in building a fractional practice is positioning. Not the tagline on your LinkedIn profile — the substantive answer to the question: what kind of company, at what stage, with what kind of problem, are you best positioned to help?

The instinct of most experienced operators is to answer this question broadly. "I have done operations, finance, and people management across multiple sectors and stages. I can help a wide range of companies." This is true of most operators with fifteen years of experience, and it is precisely why it is useless as a positioning statement. A founder who is looking for a fractional COO does not want a generalist. They want someone who has solved the specific problem they have — at their stage, in their sector, with constraints similar to their own. The broader your positioning, the less any specific founder believes you are the right person for their specific situation.

The discipline of positioning is narrowing until it feels uncomfortable. Not "operations and growth" but "Series A SaaS companies that have just crossed SGD 5M ARR and need to build the finance and ops infrastructure before their next raise." Not "strategic leadership for startups" but "fractional COO for consumer startups going through their first hiring scale-up." The narrower the positioning, the easier the sales conversation — because the prospect immediately understands whether they are in your target zone, and those who are will find you far more credible than a generalist.

The fear of narrowing is that you will exclude potential clients. You will. That is the point. A practice built on referrals and warm inbound — which is the right model for a fractional in the first two years — grows faster when every client you serve is well-matched to your positioning, because they refer people like them. The client who is a bad fit refers people who are also a bad fit. The client who is a great fit refers people who are exactly the kind of company you want to work with next.

Pipeline: the work that does not feel like work until it is too late.

Pipeline is the part of a fractional practice that most operators neglect until they are in a cash flow problem. The pattern is predictable: you are deep in an engagement, delivering good work, and the pipeline activity drops to near zero because there is not enough time for both. When the engagement ends — or when a client does not renew — there is nothing in the pipeline, and the frantic activity that follows produces clients who are either a poor fit or negotiated at below-market rates because urgency has compromised your ability to be selective.

The solution is not complicated but it requires discipline: treat pipeline maintenance as non-negotiable overhead. Not when you have time. Not when a client ends. Consistently, as a fixed allocation of your weekly operating time. For most fractionals at the one-to-two-client stage, this means four to six hours per week dedicated to relationship maintenance, visibility work, and active outreach. Not sales calls — relationship calls. Not pitches — conversations with people who might know someone you should talk to in three months.

The mechanics of pipeline for a fractional practice are different from those of a sales team. Cold outreach works poorly because the trust required for a fractional engagement is high. Inbound from content and visibility works, but slowly. The most reliable pipeline source for most fractionals in the first two years is warm referrals from existing and former clients, combined with active presence in the communities where their target companies congregate. This means being known — actually known, not just visible — in the right circles. It means being the person that someone's investor mentions when the founder says they need operational help at Series A. That kind of reputation takes time to build and is almost impossible to build while deep in a single engagement with no bandwidth for anything else.

Pricing: why you are almost certainly too cheap.

Fractional operators systematically underprice their services in the first year. The reasons are predictable: uncertainty about what the market will bear, reluctance to lose an opportunity on price, a deeply internalized frame from employment where compensation was set by someone else rather than negotiated as a business term. The result is engagements that are structurally sound but financially unsustainable — not enough revenue per client to justify the overhead of running a practice across multiple clients.

The reference point that most operators use — their last full-time salary, divided by the days they are working — is the wrong frame. A fractional engagement carries a different cost structure than full-time employment. The client is not paying CPF. They are not providing office space, equipment, or the other overhead of employment. More importantly, they are buying senior leverage on a defined problem with a defined timeframe — not committing to the long-term employment relationship that a full-time hire represents. The value delivered per dollar is different, and the pricing should reflect that.

The retainer model — a fixed monthly fee for a defined engagement scope — is almost always better than hourly pricing. It is better for the client because they know their cost and can plan around it. It is better for the fractional because it creates predictable revenue, removes the implicit incentive to maximize hours, and makes the conversation about value rather than time. The discipline is scoping the retainer correctly — not so tight that the operator cannot do good work, not so loose that the engagement scope expands to fill the available time without additional compensation.

The fractional who treats their practice like a career will be perpetually surprised by the gaps between engagements. The one who treats it like a business will build the pipeline before they need it and price their services based on value delivered, not hours billed.

The operational infrastructure you need before you think you need it.

Most new fractionals spend almost no time building the operational infrastructure of their practice — contracts, invoicing, client communication systems, knowledge management — until they are forced to by a specific problem. A client dispute that would have been prevented by a clear scope-of-work clause. An invoice that is sixty days late because there was no standard payment term. A situation where the fractional cannot remember what they agreed to with which client because everything was verbal.

The infrastructure is not glamorous but it is the difference between a professional services business and a series of informal consulting arrangements. At minimum: a standard engagement letter with clear scope, deliverables, payment terms, and termination conditions. A simple invoicing system that creates a record of what has been paid and what is outstanding. A one-page operating agreement for each client that captures the weekly rhythm — when you are available, how decisions get made, what the communication channels are. These are not bureaucratic burdens. They are the operating system that allows you to manage multiple clients without the overhead growing proportionally to the client count.

The knowledge management question deserves particular attention. As a full-time operator, the institutional knowledge you accumulate stays in the company. As a fractional, you accumulate knowledge across multiple companies and contexts, and that knowledge compounds — pattern recognition about what works in which situations, frameworks that you have tested and refined across different operating environments. The operator who captures this systematically is building a durable intellectual asset that makes each subsequent engagement more valuable. The one who keeps it in their head is building nothing that survives their own memory capacity.

The first year of a fractional practice is harder than most operators expect. The combination of doing the work and building the business — simultaneously, with limited runway — is genuinely demanding. The operators who make it through year one with a sustainable book of business are the ones who treated the practice as the thing they were building, not just the engagements they were running. The difference between those two orientations is the difference between a consultant who is always one client loss away from a cash flow problem and one who has built something that compounds.

  • 01
    Narrow your positioning until it feels uncomfortable. "Operators who have seen a lot" is not positioning. "Fractional COO for Series A SaaS companies going through their first ops build-out" is. The narrower the target, the stronger the signal that the right client gets when they encounter you, and the better the referral quality from clients you serve well.
  • 02
    Treat pipeline as non-negotiable overhead. Four to six hours per week, every week, regardless of how busy the current engagements are. Relationship maintenance, community presence, and active outreach to referral sources. This is the activity that prevents the boom-bust cycle that ends most fractional practices in year two.
  • 03
    Price for value, not time. Use retainers, not hourly rates. The conversation about value is more honest and more favorable than the conversation about hours. A well-scoped monthly retainer aligns incentives for both parties. If you are still pricing hourly after six months, figure out why and fix it.
  • 04
    Build the operating infrastructure before you need it. Standard engagement letter. Clear payment terms. A communication operating agreement for each client. A simple invoicing system. These are not burdens — they are the operating system that makes managing multiple clients possible without proportional overhead growth.
  • 05
    Capture what you learn across clients. The pattern recognition you accumulate across engagements is the intellectual asset that makes you more valuable over time. Build a system for capturing it — what worked, what did not, what the early signals were — and review it regularly. The operator who does this is compounding. The one who keeps it in their head is not.
  • 06
    Be selective about your first clients, even if it is painful. A bad-fit first client poisons the pipeline with bad-fit referrals, consumes disproportionate energy, and produces a case study that attracts the wrong next prospect. It is better to run a six-week unpaid project for the right client than a six-month paid engagement for the wrong one.

Frequently asked questions.

How long does it take to build a full fractional practice from scratch?

Eighteen months is the honest answer for most operators. The first three months are spent getting the first client and discovering what your positioning actually needs to be in practice. Months four through nine involve refining your offer, building pipeline discipline, and learning how to manage multiple clients without degrading quality. By month twelve, most operators who are going to make the transition work have a stable two or three-client book and a pipeline they trust. The final six months of the first full year are about optimizing — pricing, scope, client selection, and the operational infrastructure that makes it sustainable.

What is the biggest mistake operators make when pricing their fractional services?

Pricing for time rather than for value. An operator who charges SGD 200 per hour for two days per week is thinking like a contractor. An operator who charges SGD 8,000 per month for a defined engagement scope is thinking like a fractional. The monthly retainer model is structurally better for both parties: the client knows their cost, the operator knows their revenue, and neither party is incentivized to optimize for or against hours. Operators who start with hourly pricing almost always move to retainers within six months — after learning the hard way that hours are the wrong unit.

How do you find the first two clients as a fractional operator?

From your existing network, not from cold outreach. The first client almost always comes from a former employer, a former colleague who moved to a new company, or a referral from someone who has watched you operate. The second client usually comes from the first. This is not a permanent strategy — eventually you need a pipeline that does not depend entirely on relationship proximity — but it is the right starting point because it gives you a client who already understands your capabilities and a case study you can use to build credibility with future clients.

How many clients can a fractional operator handle at once without burning out?

Two is comfortable. Three is manageable with good operational infrastructure. Four is the edge for most operators, and beyond four the quality of the work degrades noticeably. The constraint is not calendar time — it is the cognitive load of holding the context for multiple companies simultaneously. Each client needs you to know their business, their team, their current challenges, and their operating rhythm. Four is about the limit of what most people can hold in working memory without dropping things. The operators who manage more than four clients have usually made significant trade-offs in engagement depth.
