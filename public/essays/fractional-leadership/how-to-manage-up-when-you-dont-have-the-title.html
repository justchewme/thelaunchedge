How to manage up when you don't have the title.

A fractional operator's most important skill has nothing to do with the function they were hired for. It is the ability to influence decisions above their nominal pay grade — without the title, the equity alignment, or the formal authority that a full-time executive would bring to the same conversation.

The authority problem that no one talks about in the pitch.

When a founder hires a fractional operator, the conversation is usually about functional expertise. They need someone who has built a demand generation engine before, or who has managed a 40-person ops team through a rapid scaling phase, or who knows how to run a finance function that will survive a due diligence process. The functional expertise is what gets you the engagement. It is not what makes the engagement work.

What makes the engagement work is the ability to change the behavior of people who do not report to you — starting with the CEO. This is the authority problem that no one names in the pitch, because naming it would require acknowledging that authority, in most organizations, is not derived from the contract you signed. It is derived from a combination of track record, relationship, institutional knowledge, and organizational position. A fractional operator starts with partial access to one of those four inputs and none of the others.

The operators who figure this out quickly understand that managing up is not a supplementary skill for when things get political. It is the primary skill. Everything else — the operational expertise, the frameworks, the pattern recognition — is only as valuable as your ability to get the CEO to act on it. If the CEO is not making decisions differently because of you, the engagement is failing regardless of what the deliverables say.

The techniques that work for managing up as a fractional are different from what works for a full-time executive, because the inputs are different. A CFO who has been with the company for three years manages up through a combination of demonstrated judgment, organizational loyalty, and a relationship that includes enough accumulated trust to survive disagreement. A fractional operator has none of those by default. They have to build trust faster, on a shorter runway, using different tools.

Frame decisions as options, not recommendations.

The single most effective technique for managing up without authority is presenting options rather than conclusions. This sounds like a small thing. It is not.

When you present a recommendation, you create a binary dynamic: the CEO either agrees with you or does not. If they disagree, the implicit subtext is that your judgment was wrong. Over time, a pattern of CEO rejections accumulates into a credibility problem for the operator, even when the underlying recommendations were sound. The CEO starts to see the operator as someone who needs to be managed rather than someone who is helping them think.

When you present three options — each with honest trade-offs, costs, and risks, and with a clearly recommended path — you are doing something structurally different. You are demonstrating that you understand the decision space rather than just having a preference. You are giving the CEO information they need rather than asking them to approve your conclusion. And you are creating a context where a disagreement with your recommendation is not a rejection of your judgment — it is a different weighting of the trade-offs, which is legitimate. The CEO who rejects option A and picks option B still trusted you enough to use your framing. That is a win.

There is a version of this technique that is manipulative — presenting three options where two are obviously bad so the CEO inevitably lands on your preferred one. That approach works exactly once. CEOs are not naive, and the ones who figure out they are being steered will stop sharing information with you. The discipline is presenting genuinely good options with honest assessments of each, including the option you do not prefer. Trust is built by making the CEO's decisions better, not by making your preferred outcome inevitable.

Build the trust account through small predictions.

Trust, for a fractional, is not built through relationship history. It is built through a series of demonstrated judgments that turned out to be correct. Every time you say "if we launch this product without the support documentation, the first 200 signups will churn within 30 days" and that is what happens, you make a deposit into the trust account. Every time you are wrong about something — especially something you stated with confidence — you make a withdrawal.

The implication is that the early months of an engagement should be oriented around making predictions you can verify quickly. Not because short-term predictions are more important than long-term ones, but because a short feedback loop allows the trust account to build faster. A prediction that resolves in three weeks is more valuable to your credibility position than one that resolves in a year, even if the long-term one is more consequential for the business.

This also means being disciplined about confidence levels. A fractional who consistently overstates their certainty — who says "this will definitely work" when what they mean is "this has a 70% chance of working" — is creating a trust problem every time the 30% case materializes. The CEOs who work best with fractional operators are usually the ones who respect calibrated uncertainty. Being right about what you do not know is as important as being right about what you do.

Create accountability without confrontation.

One of the practical challenges of managing up as a fractional is that you cannot use most of the normal accountability mechanisms. You cannot put the CEO on a performance improvement plan. You cannot escalate to their manager. You cannot make their compensation contingent on following through on commitments. When the CEO says they will do something and does not do it, your options are limited.

The technique that works is written documentation combined with short follow-up cycles. After every significant meeting where a decision is made or a commitment is taken on, send a brief summary — two or three sentences — of what was decided and who owns what. Not a formal meeting minutes document, which creates bureaucratic friction. A short, direct message: "Following our conversation today: we are pausing the agency partnership until Q3, you will notify them by end of week, and we will revisit in June based on the pipeline numbers." Then, when the follow-up moment arrives, the reference point exists.

The reason this works is that it removes the interpersonal friction from accountability. The CEO who did not send the notification by end of week is not being accused of failing — they are being reminded of their own decision. The written record is not a trap. It is a shared memory that makes follow-through easier for everyone. Most people, including most CEOs, appreciate this more than they resist it, once they understand you are not documenting to catch them out but to make the operating system of the engagement actually work.

The fractional operator who can influence a CEO's decisions without formal authority is more valuable than one who has the title and cannot get anyone to act.

The conversations that require something different.

The techniques above work for the routine operating rhythm of managing up. Framing options, building trust through accurate predictions, creating written accountability loops — these are the mechanics of a functional engagement. They do not fully address the situations where something more confrontational is required.

When the CEO is about to make a decision that you believe is genuinely costly — a hire, a pricing move, a market bet — and the option-framing approach has not changed their trajectory, the managed-up conversation has to become more direct. This is distinct from the "tell the CEO they are wrong" scenario that deserves its own treatment. This is about the situations where the CEO is moving fast on something and your job is to slow them down enough to examine it properly.

The language that works in these moments is specific: "Before we move forward, I want to make sure we have accounted for [X]. If I am wrong about [X], I am comfortable with the decision. If I am right about it, the cost could be [Y]. Can we take 48 hours to verify?" This framing does several things simultaneously. It identifies the crux rather than the entire decision. It acknowledges the possibility that you are wrong — which reduces defensiveness. It asks for a short pause rather than a reversal, which is a much easier ask. And it ties the verification to the specific risk, so the CEO knows exactly what would change your view.

Fractionals who can execute this kind of conversation consistently — quickly, clearly, without drama — are in a different category from those who manage up only in smooth conditions. The high-leverage moments in any engagement are the ones where the CEO is moving fast in a direction that is costly, and the fractional's job is to be the force that slows the decision down without becoming an obstacle to the organization's momentum.

  • 01
    Present options, not recommendations. Frame every significant decision as a set of genuine alternatives with honest trade-offs. The CEO who chooses option B over your preferred option A has still benefited from your thinking — and you have not created a win-lose dynamic that erodes trust over time.
  • 02
    Build trust through short-cycle predictions. Make claims you can verify quickly and be right about them. Calibrate your confidence levels accurately — overstating certainty creates trust problems every time the uncertain case materializes. The trust account is built incrementally, not through one dramatic win.
  • 03
    Write every commitment down. After meetings where decisions are made, send a brief summary of what was decided and who owns what. This is not bureaucracy — it is the infrastructure that makes accountability possible without confrontation.
  • 04
    Find the crux, not the whole decision. When you need to slow a CEO down on a costly move, identify the specific assumption that, if wrong, changes everything. Ask to verify that assumption rather than asking to revisit the decision. A narrower ask is easier to grant.
  • 05
    Never manage up through the team. Using the team's sentiment to pressure the CEO is the fastest way to destroy a fractional engagement. If the team is unhappy with a direction, your job is to surface that directly to the CEO — not to let it percolate into a coalition. Operators who work through back channels do not last.
  • 06
    Know when to defer. Not every decision is worth the political capital. An operator who manages up on every disagreement trains the CEO to expect friction, which makes them less receptive when the high-stakes conversations actually arrive. Save the influence for the decisions where the downside of being wrong is real.

Frequently asked questions.

What does managing up mean for a fractional operator?

Managing up, in this context, means influencing decisions made by the CEO or board that you do not have formal authority over. For a fractional, this includes budget decisions, hiring, strategic pivots, and priority trade-offs. The tools available are different from those available to a full-time executive — you cannot rely on organizational authority, equity alignment, or long-term relationship capital in the same way. You rely instead on the quality of your reasoning, the trust you have built, and the discipline of your framing.

How do you build the CEO's trust as a fractional without a long track record together?

Through a series of low-stakes predictions that turn out to be correct. Every time you say "if we do X, Y will happen" and it does, you deposit into the trust account. The first few of these need to be in areas where you have high confidence and where the feedback loop is short — so the CEO can see the outcome within weeks, not quarters. This is why choosing the right early initiatives matters so much. You are not just delivering outcomes. You are demonstrating pattern recognition.

What is the most common mistake fractionals make when managing up?

Presenting conclusions instead of options. When a fractional arrives with a recommendation and asks the CEO to approve it, they have created a binary: yes or no. A no means the fractional loses, which creates a defensive dynamic over time. When the fractional presents three options with a clearly recommended path and an honest account of the trade-offs, the CEO is making a choice rather than rendering a verdict. The outcome is often the same, but the dynamic is entirely different.

How do you create accountability with a CEO when you have no formal authority?

Write things down and refer back to them. When a CEO makes a decision in a meeting, send a brief written summary of what was decided, what the next step is, and who owns it. When you follow up a week later, you are not creating bureaucracy — you are creating a record that makes accountability possible without confrontation. The CEO who does not follow through on their own written commitments has a harder time dismissing the follow-up than one whose commitments only exist in memory.
