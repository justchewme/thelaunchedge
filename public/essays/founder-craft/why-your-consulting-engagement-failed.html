Why your consulting engagement failed before it started.

The failure wasn't in the delivery. It was in the room before the contract was signed — in the brief that wasn't written, the outcome that wasn't named, and the decision-maker who was never in the call. Here's what we've learned watching engagements collapse from both sides of the table.

The moment it breaks.

There is a specific moment in every failed consulting engagement where the outcome became inevitable. It is not the missed deadline, the off-target deliverable, or the founder who stops responding to Slack. Those are symptoms. The moment is earlier — usually in the first conversation, sometimes in the brief, always before the invoice is raised.

We have been on both sides of this. As operators inside the room with a consultant we hired, watching money leave the account while the work drifted. And as the practice taking a brief, realising halfway through a scoping call that the client had not yet decided what they wanted to be true in twelve weeks. In every case, the failure was baked in before the work started.

The pattern is consistent enough that we can describe it in four moves. Understanding them doesn't make you immune — but it makes the failure harder to miss.

Move one: hiring a type instead of an outcome.

The first move happens when a founder decides they need "a strategy consultant" or "a GTM advisor" or "a fractional CMO" before they have defined what problem those words are supposed to solve. The category is hired. The outcome is not.

This is more common in Singapore and Southeast Asia than it sounds. The ecosystem here runs on warm introductions and pattern-matching — someone hired a fractional CFO and it went well, so the next founder hires a fractional CFO. The consultant is credentialed, the references check out, the first meeting goes well. The engagement starts without anyone in the room having written down what success looks like in week ten.

The consultant then does consultant things. They diagnose, they framework, they produce. The founder receives work product and realises, slowly, that it is not the thing that was stuck. The consultant was solving the right problem for a different company. Nobody is lying. Nobody is lazy. The scope just wasn't written in a way that forced anyone to test the assumption.

The category was hired. The outcome was not. And nobody in that first call thought to ask: what would have to be true in twelve weeks for this to have been worth it?

Move two: the absent decision-maker.

The second move: the person who signs the invoice and the person who attends the working sessions are different people. This is almost always fatal.

We have run engagements in Jakarta, Kuala Lumpur, and Bangkok where the founder delegated the consulting relationship to a senior manager — usually for legitimate reasons: they were raising, they were managing a crisis, they trusted the team. The working sessions happened. The diagnostics were thorough. The recommendations were solid. And then the founder read the final memo and said "this isn't what I wanted" — not because the work was wrong, but because they had never been in the room where the scope was set.

The correct posture: the decision-maker must be present for the first two sessions and the final review, at minimum. Not to approve every output — that micromanagement kills good operators — but to calibrate what "right" means early enough that it can still be course-corrected. An operator who can't get thirty minutes of the founder's calendar in week one cannot produce an outcome the founder will trust in week ten.

Move three: confusing delivery with work.

The third move is structural and almost invisible until it's too late. The consultant delivers a document. The founder accepts the document. The engagement ends. Nothing changes inside the company.

This is the consulting industry's oldest sleight of hand, and it works because documents feel like outcomes. A 60-page strategy deck, a market-entry report, a competitive analysis — these are real work. They take real time to produce. They look like value. But a document that lives on a SharePoint and is referenced in zero subsequent decisions is not an outcome. It is theatre performed in the past tense.

The test we apply to every engagement before we scope it: what will be running in the client's systems at close-out? A live funnel. A hired operator. An AI agent on their VPS handling a specific workflow. If the answer is "a document," we push back until we find the thing behind the document — because there is always a thing behind the document, and that thing is what the founder actually needs.

Move four: scope that drifts.

The fourth move happens after the engagement starts. The scope was clear at signing. Then the founder mentions a second problem in week three. Then a third. The consultant — who wants to be helpful, who is in the room and can see the problems — starts addressing them. The original outcome recedes. The engagement expands sideways. By week eight, everyone is working hard on things that were never in the brief.

The antidote is not rigidity — good operators adapt. It is explicit renegotiation. Every time the scope shifts materially, a new memo is written. A new outcome is agreed. The old one is either closed out or explicitly de-prioritised. This sounds bureaucratic. In practice it takes forty-five minutes and a short email, and it means that the final engagement review is never a surprise.

The practices and consultancies that do the best work over time are the ones that have normalised saying "that's outside the current scope — let's decide together whether to extend or address it in a follow-on." It is not a refusal to help. It is a commitment to finishing the thing they started.

The six things a good brief must contain.

  • 01
    A measurable outcome. One sentence. Not "improve activation" — "lift activation from 22% to 38% on new cohorts within 10 weeks." If you cannot write the outcome in one sentence, you have not finished scoping the work.
  • 02
    Calendar dates, not durations. "Eight weeks" becomes twelve. "Start: 5 May, close-out: 30 June" does not. Every milestone gets a calendar date. Drift is harder to ignore when it has a specific date attached.
  • 03
    What the client provides. Data access, system credentials, a named decision-maker who attends the first two sessions, and an honest account of what has already been tried. A consultant who starts without this is navigating blind.
  • 04
    What is shipped, and where. Not a document — a live artefact in the client's systems. A funnel that runs. A workflow that fires. A hire who has started. The output must outlive the engagement.
  • 05
    A fixed price. Hourly billing rewards time spent, not outcomes reached. A fixed fee — paid in two tranches, no escalators — aligns both sides on getting to done. If the work is bigger than estimated, that cost belongs to the operator who underscoped it.
  • 06
    Explicit exclusions. What the consultant will not do. This is the most important section because it prevents the scope from drifting into adjacent work that looks helpful but isn't what was agreed. "We will not run the all-hands. We will not represent the company externally. We will not write board materials."

Frequently asked questions.

What makes a consulting engagement fail?

Most engagements fail because the scope was unclear from the start — there was no agreed outcome, no named decision-maker, and no fixed definition of what "done" meant. The consultant delivered something; it just wasn't what the client actually needed.

How do I hire a good consultant in Singapore or SEA?

Ask them to write the outcome in one sentence before you sign anything. If they can't — or produce a paragraph of hedged language — they haven't scoped the work. The best operators in Singapore will give you a two-page memo with a fixed quote before the engagement starts.

What should a consulting scope include?

A good scope covers: the measurable outcome, the timeline in calendar dates, what the client provides, what the consultant delivers into the client's systems, the fixed price, and explicitly what the consultant will not do. Six items. Two pages.

How much does a fractional consultant cost in Singapore?

Good fractional operators in Singapore typically work on fixed-fee engagements scoped after an intro call. Retainers for two-to-three days per week range from SGD 15,000 to SGD 40,000 per month depending on seniority and scope. Anyone offering hourly billing for strategic work is selling time, not outcomes.
