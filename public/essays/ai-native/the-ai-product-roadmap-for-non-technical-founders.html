The AI product roadmap for non-technical founders

You are not at a disadvantage because you cannot read the code. You are at a disadvantage because you do not yet have a framework for evaluating what engineers tell you about AI work — what is realistic, what is being oversimplified, and what questions to ask before you commit to a feature that is six weeks of engineering and impossible to scope properly.

The problem is not technical literacy. It is a missing decision framework.

Non-technical founders often assume their problem with AI is that they do not understand the technology. This is rarely true. The founders I work with understand their users exceptionally well. They understand their business constraints. They understand what a good outcome looks like. What they are missing is not technical knowledge — it is a framework for applying their existing judgment to AI decisions.

Technical founders have an analogous blind spot: they understand the technology but frequently misjudge the user problem it is being applied to. The non-technical founder's judgment is often more accurate on the product question. The gap is on the implementation question — not whether an AI feature is a good idea, but whether the proposed scope is realistic, whether the vendor is selling something that works in production, and whether the engineering estimate reflects the actual complexity of the work.

This essay is about building that framework. Not about understanding transformers or embeddings or context windows. About the questions to ask, the signals to look for, and the decisions that are actually yours to make regardless of your technical background.

Build vs. buy: the question that actually matters.

The first decision on any AI feature is whether you are building it or buying it. In most startup contexts, the right answer is to buy until there is a specific reason you cannot. The cost of building on top of foundation models has collapsed dramatically. Most common AI functionality — classification, summarisation, question-answering over documents, structured data extraction — can be implemented in days using existing APIs rather than weeks using custom model work.

The reasons to build rather than buy are narrower than most engineering teams will tell you. You build when the vendor's data handling is incompatible with your privacy requirements. You build when the task is sufficiently specific to your domain that no existing vendor solution is close enough. You build when you have strong evidence that the cost of the API at your scale will exceed the cost of a custom solution — and you should demand evidence for this claim before accepting it.

The most common mistake is building because it feels more defensible or because an engineer prefers it. Building a custom embedding pipeline for a use case that GPT-4o with retrieval would have covered adequately is four weeks of engineering that did not move the product forward. The defensibility argument is almost always premature: you need evidence of product-market fit before the question of what is defensible becomes relevant.

When evaluating buy options, the questions to ask are: what is the accuracy on my specific use case, not their benchmark? What does error handling look like? What is the migration path if I need to switch? What are the contractual data handling obligations? A vendor who cannot answer the error handling question clearly is not ready for production.

Evaluating LLM vendors without a technical background.

LLM vendor evaluation is an area where the non-technical founder's intuition breaks down most completely, because the demos are universally impressive and the failure modes are invisible until you are in production. Here is a practical approach that does not require technical depth.

First, never evaluate a vendor using their own demo. Bring your five hardest cases — the queries your users actually ask that are most likely to break an AI system. Ask the vendor to run those. Watch what happens when the system fails. The failure mode tells you more than any success case.

Second, ask specifically about the latency on your use case at your expected volume. AI inference is expensive and slow relative to traditional software. A 3-second response time is acceptable for a document summarisation task. It is not acceptable for an in-conversation support bot where users expect near-instant replies. Vendors will give you benchmarks from optimal conditions. Ask for P95 latency — the response time at the 95th percentile of requests — under realistic load.

Third, ask for reference customers who have been in production for more than 12 months, not recent launches. Twelve months of production reveals the stale knowledge base problem, the model deprecation problem, and the cost-at-scale problem. Recent launches reveal none of these things. If the vendor cannot provide 12-month references in your industry, weight that heavily.

You do not need to understand how the model works. You need to understand what happens when it is wrong — and whether the vendor has designed their product around that reality.

How to scope AI features without being misled.

The scoping problem is where non-technical founders lose the most time and money. An engineer tells you an AI feature will take three weeks. It takes twelve. The estimate was wrong not because the engineer was incompetent but because AI feature scoping has failure modes that do not exist in traditional software development.

The first failure mode is data dependency underestimation. Most AI features require input data in a specific format. The data you have is not in that format. Cleaning, structuring, and validating the data is not counted in the engineering estimate because the engineer assumed it was done or simple. It is almost never simple. Ask specifically: what data does this feature require as input, and where does that data come from today? If the answer is "we will pull it from the CRM," ask when that data was last audited for quality.

The second failure mode is accuracy threshold ambiguity. "Good enough" accuracy is defined differently by different people. A feature that classifies customer feedback into categories and is accurate 85% of the time sounds reasonable. If your team is using that classification to route support tickets, 15% misclassification means 15% of your support tickets go to the wrong team and generate a second contact. Define the acceptable accuracy threshold before you commit to building, and make the engineer state explicitly whether they believe that threshold is achievable.

The third failure mode is evaluation absence. Most AI feature engineering estimates do not include time to build an evaluation pipeline — a way to systematically measure whether the feature is working. Without an evaluation pipeline, you will not know the feature is degrading until users report problems. Evaluation pipelines are not glamorous, and engineers frequently omit them from estimates because they are not strictly required to ship. They are required to know whether what you shipped is working.

Prioritising AI work against everything else.

The most common failure mode I see in AI-forward startup roadmaps is AI work that never gets de-prioritised even when it is not working. The initial investment creates a sunk cost that makes it hard to admit the feature is not delivering. The AI features sit on the roadmap, consuming engineering time, while the core product work that would actually drive growth waits.

The discipline required is the same as for any other roadmap prioritisation: define the success criteria before you start, not after. What metric is this AI feature moving? By how much? By when? If you cannot define a measurable outcome upfront, that is a signal the feature is not ready to be on the roadmap. It belongs in exploration, with a time-boxed budget, not in committed delivery.

AI features also have a specific prioritisation risk: they are exciting to build and to talk about, which creates pressure to prioritise them over less exciting but more important work. A well-implemented AI feature that 20% of users engage with generates far less business value than a core product improvement that affects 100% of user sessions. Keep AI features in competition with everything else, and hold them to the same evidence standard.

  • 01
    Default to buying, not building. Most AI functionality available via API is good enough for the use case and costs a fraction of custom engineering. Build only when there is a specific, articulable reason the API solution is inadequate — not because building feels more defensible.
  • 02
    Evaluate vendors on failure cases, not demos. Bring your five hardest real-world queries. Watch what happens when the system fails. Ask specifically about error handling, fallback behaviour, and what users experience when the output is wrong.
  • 03
    Define the accuracy threshold before you commit to build. Ask what accuracy level is required for this feature to be useful, whether the engineer believes that is achievable, and how it will be measured. If these questions cannot be answered, the feature is not ready to scope.
  • 04
    Add data cleaning time to every AI estimate. Assume the data is not in the format you need. Ask where each piece of input data comes from, when it was last cleaned, and who owns it. Data preparation consistently takes two to three times longer than engineers initially estimate.
  • 05
    Require an evaluation pipeline as part of every AI feature. If there is no way to measure whether the feature is working, you do not know whether it is working. Evaluation pipelines are not optional — they are the mechanism by which you know the investment delivered its intended outcome.

Frequently asked questions.

How do I evaluate an AI vendor without a technical co-founder?

Ask the vendor to walk you through a failure case — not just a success case. Ask what happens when the output is wrong, how you would detect it, and what the remediation path looks like. Ask for reference customers in your industry who have been running the product for more than 12 months, not recent launches. Vendors who cannot answer these questions clearly are not ready for production deployment.

Should AI features go on the main product roadmap or a separate one?

On the main roadmap, competing for priority with everything else. Keeping AI work on a separate roadmap is how it becomes a skunkworks project that never ships or a priority sinkhole that never gets de-prioritised even when it is not working. AI features should be held to the same success criteria as any other product feature: a clear user problem, a measurable outcome, and a defined owner.

What is the biggest AI scoping mistake founders make?

Committing to AI-generated outputs without a plan for when the output is wrong. The most common failure pattern is a founder who sees a demo, assumes 95% accuracy means 5% edge cases they can ignore, and ships. In production, 5% error on high-stakes outputs — customer quotes, medical information, legal summaries — is significant. Every AI feature needs a defined error handling path before it goes into the roadmap.

How much of my engineering budget should go to AI features?

There is no universal rule, but a useful constraint is this: do not let AI feature work consume more than 30% of engineering bandwidth until at least one AI feature has shipped, been in production for 90 days, and demonstrated a measurable outcome. The first AI feature teaches you more about what is actually hard than any planning session will. Build that learning into your budget before you scale the investment.
