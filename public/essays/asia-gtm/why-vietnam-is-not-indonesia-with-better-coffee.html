Why Vietnam is not Indonesia with better coffee

In pitch decks and strategy documents, Vietnam and Indonesia appear as near-siblings — large young populations, rapidly growing middle classes, underpenetrated digital markets ripe for disruption. In practice, they are structurally different economies that punish identical playbooks. The operators who succeed in both have built separate mental models for each market, not a single "emerging Asia" framework that slides between them.

The geography error.

Indonesia is an archipelago of more than 17,000 islands, spread across roughly 5,000 kilometres of ocean. Vietnam is a narrow coastal strip — 1,650 kilometres long but rarely more than 500 kilometres wide — that runs along the eastern edge of the Indochinese peninsula. These are not just geographic curiosities. They determine, at a fundamental level, how distribution works in each country, how the economy is structured, and what the practical ceiling is for a business that does not build for the specific geography of each market.

In Indonesia, the distribution challenge is a logistics and cost problem. Getting a physical product from Jakarta to Makassar requires either air freight (expensive), sea freight (slow, weather-dependent, often unreliable), or a combination of both. The result is that product prices rise significantly as you move east — a jar of peanut butter that costs IDR 25,000 in a Jakarta supermarket might cost IDR 40,000 in a Kupang warung, if it is available at all. For digital products, this geography still matters: internet infrastructure quality degrades significantly outside Java and Bali, which affects user experience and limits the addressable market for products that require reliable connectivity.

In Vietnam, the north-south divide is the operational fault line, and it is cultural as much as geographic. Ho Chi Minh City (the south) is commercially oriented, entrepreneurially energetic, and historically more open to foreign business models. Hanoi (the north) is the political capital, home to state-owned enterprises and government ministries, and operates at a more conservative and bureaucratic pace. A business that launches in HCMC and assumes it will scale naturally to Hanoi is making an error. The cities are separated by 1,700 kilometres of road and a cultural gap that matters enormously for enterprise sales, regulatory relationships, and hiring.

Single-party state vs. messy democracy: what this actually means operationally.

Vietnam is governed by the Communist Party of Vietnam, which maintains authority over regulatory approvals, business licensing, data governance, and foreign investment rules through a centralised structure. This does not mean Vietnam is closed to foreign business — it attracts tens of billions of dollars in FDI each year, largely in manufacturing and technology. What it means is that the regulatory environment is politically shaped in ways that are not always visible from the outside, and that policy can shift rapidly when it conflicts with state priorities.

Operators who have navigated Vietnamese regulation describe a system that is simultaneously more legible and less predictable than Indonesia's. More legible because there is a single national authority structure — you are not dealing with Jakarta regulations and West Java regulations and Banten regulations simultaneously. Less predictable because a policy decision made at the party level can reorganise entire industries without the democratic deliberation and public comment periods that make Indonesian regulatory change (slowly) telegraphed. In 2023, Vietnam issued Decree 13/2023 on personal data protection with a short implementation timeline and requirements that surprised many foreign operators who had not been tracking the drafting process. This kind of surprise is structurally less common in Indonesia, where regulatory reform involves multiple stakeholders, legislative processes, and typically leaks well in advance.

Indonesia's regulatory environment is, by contrast, highly decentralised, with both national regulations and regional government authority (often called Perda, or Peraturan Daerah — regional regulations). A fintech product that is compliant with OJK (Indonesia's financial services authority) national requirements may still require separate registrations or modifications to operate in specific provinces. For consumer internet businesses, this complexity is often manageable. For businesses that touch financial services, healthcare, education, or logistics, the regulatory stack can be genuinely complicated — sometimes requiring three to five separate approvals from different bodies before commercial launch is legally clean.

How consumers evaluate trust — and what role foreign brands play.

Vietnamese urban consumers, particularly in Ho Chi Minh City, have a demonstrable preference for foreign brands in premium and aspirational categories. This is not brand snobbery in the way the word implies in Western markets. It is a rational response to decades in which domestic production was state-controlled, quality was inconsistent, and foreign goods were scarce and therefore valued. The consumer who prefers a Korean cosmetic brand or a Japanese appliance brand to a Vietnamese equivalent is not being irrational — they are applying a learned quality heuristic that has, historically, been reliable.

For foreign brands entering Vietnam, this is both an advantage and a trap. The advantage is that "foreign origin" carries credibility, and premium pricing is achievable in categories where this heuristic applies. The trap is that the brand benefit is category-dependent and cohort-dependent. It applies more strongly to Gen X and older millennials in urban Vietnam than to Gen Z consumers who have grown up with sophisticated Vietnamese brands like Highlands Coffee and a new generation of Vietnamese DTC brands that have closed the quality gap. Assuming "foreign brand premium" is available to you across all segments and categories is a market research failure.

Indonesian consumers, by contrast, have demonstrated rapid adoption of domestically-built brands that match on quality and undercut on price. Kopi Kenangan built one of the country's largest coffee chains within a few years of launch. Wardah became one of Indonesia's leading cosmetics brands by building for local consumers and distribution preferences. The Indonesian consumer is not anti-foreign. They are brand-agnostic in a way that Vietnamese consumers are not, and they will follow value and quality regardless of origin.

The founders who succeed in both Vietnam and Indonesia did not use the same playbook. They used the same discipline — deep market observation before action — and arrived at very different operational conclusions for each market.

Payment infrastructure: similar surface, different architecture.

Both Vietnam and Indonesia are predominantly cash and mobile wallet economies with low credit card penetration — only a small minority of adults use credit cards in either country. This surface similarity leads founders to assume the payment infrastructure is effectively interchangeable. It is not.

Vietnam's dominant mobile wallets are MoMo (with tens of millions of users), ZaloPay (backed by VNG, one of Vietnam's largest tech companies, and deeply integrated with the Zalo messaging app), and VNPay, which operates a QR payment network widely adopted by brick-and-mortar merchants. VNPay is particularly relevant because its QR codes can be paid from most Vietnamese banking apps without the merchant needing a separate agreement with each bank. (Indonesia's QRIS national standard does similar work, but the wallet layer on top of it is more fragmented.)

Indonesia's wallet ecosystem — GoPay, OVO, DANA, ShopeePay — is more fragmented. Each wallet has different merchant fee structures, different geographic penetration patterns (OVO is stronger in urban Java; DANA has better reach in Tier 2 and 3 cities), and different APIs for merchant integration. A payment stack built for Vietnam will not work in Indonesia. A payment strategy that assumes the two markets are interchangeable will fail in both.

The hiring difference that determines whether you can execute.

The operational differences between Vietnam and Indonesia are ultimately realised or ignored by the people you hire. And the hiring pools in each country are different in ways that matter enormously for what is actually possible in each market.

Vietnam has produced an exceptionally strong engineering talent pool, driven by decades of state investment in mathematics and technical education. Ho Chi Minh City's tech ecosystem is deep in backend engineering, mobile development, and quality assurance. Salaries are competitive — a senior software engineer in HCMC earns USD 2,000–4,000 per month, compared to USD 5,000–8,000 in Singapore for comparable roles — but the talent is genuine. The gap is in commercial and go-to-market functions. Vietnam has fewer experienced sales leaders, growth marketers, and business development operators than the engineering talent pool would suggest, because the tech ecosystem is export-oriented: Vietnamese engineers often work for foreign companies building products for non-Vietnamese markets.

Indonesia has deeper commercial talent — there are more experienced GTM operators, more people who have run sales teams at scale for domestic-facing businesses, and more operators who understand the idiosyncrasies of Indonesian distribution. The engineering talent pool is growing but thinner than Vietnam's at the senior level. The result: if you are building a product-led growth company that needs to ship product quickly in Asia, Vietnam is your engineering base. If you are building a distribution-heavy business that needs commercial operators who understand the market, Indonesia has the talent you need.

  • 01
    Build separate market entry documents for each country. A Southeast Asia market entry strategy is not a document. It is a slide that glosses over the differences that will determine whether you succeed. If your Vietnam plan and your Indonesia plan look similar, one or both of them is wrong.
  • 02
    In Vietnam, establish your regulatory relationships before commercial launch. The Ministry of Science and Technology (which absorbed the former Ministry of Information and Communications in 2025), the State Bank of Vietnam, and relevant sectoral ministries are stakeholders in your business whether you engage them or not. Engaging them proactively — through a local legal partner who has genuine relationships, not just familiarity with the filing process — gives you advance warning of regulatory changes that will otherwise surprise you.
  • 03
    In Indonesia, solve the logistics problem before you solve the marketing problem. A product that is well-marketed but unavailable or expensive in Tier 2 and 3 cities will hit a growth ceiling that no marketing budget can breach. Kino, Wings, and Unilever Indonesia have built distribution advantages that took decades and are nearly impossible to replicate quickly.
  • 04
    Never assume that success in HCMC predicts success in Hanoi. The cities are different enough that a product that resonates strongly with the commercially-oriented, risk-accepting consumer base of HCMC may land poorly in Hanoi's more conservative, government-adjacent professional class. Test both cities separately before concluding you understand Vietnam.
  • 05
    Integrate VNPay for Vietnam, GoPay and OVO for Indonesia — as separate implementations. There is no shortcut here. Payment localisation is not a one-time checkbox; it is an ongoing integration that requires separate merchant accounts, separate fee negotiations, and separate technical relationships in each market.
  • 06
    Hire your Vietnam GM for regulatory intelligence; hire your Indonesia GM for distribution reach. The competencies that determine success in each market are different. A great Vietnam country manager understands how to navigate the party-state regulatory environment. A great Indonesia country manager understands how to build distribution across 500-plus regencies. These are rarely the same person.

Frequently asked questions.

Which market is easier to enter first — Vietnam or Indonesia?

Neither is objectively easier, but they require different capabilities. Vietnam rewards operators who can navigate a state-adjacent regulatory environment and build relationships with government stakeholders. Indonesia rewards operators who can manage geographic fragmentation and distribution complexity. If you have regulatory experience and a small, concentrated team, Vietnam may be more accessible. If you have distribution expertise and capital for market development, Indonesia's scale justifies the complexity. Both require genuine local operational leadership — remote management from Singapore does not work in either market.

How does the regulatory environment differ between Vietnam and Indonesia?

Vietnam operates under a single-party state with centralised regulatory authority. Approvals from Ho Chi Minh City and Hanoi can be inconsistent, and policy can change with limited notice when it conflicts with state priorities. Indonesia is a decentralised democracy with both national and regional regulatory layers — different provinces can have different licensing requirements for the same activity. Vietnam's primary risk is political unpredictability; Indonesia's primary risk is complexity and inconsistency across 500-plus regencies. Neither risk is unmanageable, but they require different mitigation strategies.

Is the payment infrastructure similar in both countries?

Superficially similar, structurally different. Both are predominantly cash or mobile wallet economies with low credit card penetration. But Vietnam's dominant wallets — MoMo, ZaloPay, VNPay — operate on different rails and have different merchant fee structures than Indonesia's GoPay, OVO, and DANA. Vietnam also has a stronger QR code payment culture, particularly in Ho Chi Minh City and Hanoi, driven by government-backed QR standards. You need separate payment stack integrations for each market, and the integration timeline is typically 6–12 weeks per market if done properly.

Do Vietnamese consumers respond differently to foreign brands than Indonesian consumers?

Yes, and the difference is significant. Vietnamese consumers in urban areas — particularly Ho Chi Minh City — have a strong preference for foreign brands across premium categories, treating them as quality and status markers. This was partly driven by decades of state-controlled domestic production. Indonesian consumers are more brand-agnostic and will rapidly adopt local brands that match the quality of foreign alternatives, especially if the local brand prices more accessibly. The practical implication: a premium pricing strategy may be more sustainable in Vietnam; a value-plus-quality strategy often wins in Indonesia.
