The five SEA market entry mistakes that kill funded startups.

Southeast Asia has absorbed more wasted Series A capital than any other region we've operated in — not because the markets are hostile, but because the assumptions founders bring into them are catastrophically wrong. We've watched five of them cause the same damage, in the same order, across 14 markets.

Why SEA eats Western GTM playbooks.

The failure mode in Southeast Asia is almost never product-market fit. Founders who make it to a regional launch typically have a product that works. What breaks them is the GTM layer — specifically, the decision to execute a playbook that worked in San Francisco, London, or even Singapore, and apply it without modification to Jakarta, Manila, or Ho Chi Minh City.

We have been inside the room for dozens of these decisions. At foodpanda across fourteen Asian markets. At foodora across eight European ones. At a YC-backed fintech operating in 150 countries. The pattern is identical whether you are launching a fintech, a marketplace, or a B2B SaaS tool: a leadership team that has never operated in the target market makes assumptions about what customers want, how distribution works, and what a "launch" means — and then hires to execute those assumptions rather than test them.

What follows is not a framework. It is a list of the specific mistakes we have watched kill funded startups in this region, in the approximate order that they tend to occur.

Mistake one: treating SEA as a single market.

Southeast Asia is not a market. It is ten markets that share a time zone and not much else. Consumer behaviour in Indonesia is structurally different from Thailand. Payment infrastructure in Vietnam requires entirely different checkout logic than Singapore. Regulatory environments in the Philippines create friction that simply does not exist in Malaysia. Labour markets in each country have different hiring norms, different salary expectations, and different notice period conventions.

The startup that enters "Southeast Asia" is entering nothing. The startup that enters Indonesia, with a plan that accounts for the dominance of GoPay and OVO in mobile payments, the role of WhatsApp as a customer service channel, and the geographic concentration of economic activity in Java, is entering a real market. These are not decorative details. They determine whether your product reaches customers at all.

The first question we ask any founder considering a regional expansion: which country, which city, which customer segment, and which distribution channel? If any of those answers is "Southeast Asia" or "all of them," the expansion plan does not exist yet.

Mistake two: copying the Singapore playbook to Indonesia.

This is the most expensive single mistake we see. Singapore is an outlier — high income, compact geography, English-first, globally connected infrastructure, and a regulatory environment that is among the most business-friendly in the world. A product that works in Singapore works because of those conditions, not despite them. Indonesia has almost none of them.

Indonesia is 277 million people spread across 17,000 islands, with median household income roughly one-eighth of Singapore's, internet penetration concentrated in Java, a banking sector where more than 50% of adults remain underserved by traditional financial institutions, and a regulatory environment that requires local entity incorporation and, in some sectors, local ownership structures. A pricing model built for Singapore customers will be inaccessible to the vast majority of Indonesian ones. An onboarding flow built around credit card entry will lose most Indonesian users at step one.

We ran market entry in Indonesia multiple times. The playbook that works there was built in Indonesia, not adapted from elsewhere. The team that understands it is on the ground in Jakarta, not dialling in from Singapore.

Indonesia is not Singapore with a larger population and slower infrastructure. It is a different civilisation with a different economic logic — and the founders who understand that early are the ones who stay.

Mistake three: hiring a country manager before proving the model.

The sequence matters. We consistently see founders hire a country manager — senior, expensive, well-credentialed — before they have a single paying customer in the market. The logic is understandable: the hire signals commitment, it unlocks local knowledge, it lets the founder stay in Singapore and run the business. The problem is that a country manager hired before the model is proven is being asked to execute a strategy that does not yet exist.

The right sequence: founder or operator in-market for four to six weeks, first ten customers acquired through direct effort, first distribution channel tested and validated, unit economics understood for that specific market. Then you hire the country manager to scale what you have proven, not to figure out whether it works. A country manager who is also doing product discovery is doing two different jobs poorly. They will cost you the first twelve months of their tenure finding out things you should have found out before you hired them.

In the launches we have run — Malaysia, Thailand, Vietnam, Philippines, Indonesia — the engagements that succeeded fastest were the ones where a senior operator was embedded in-market for the first thirty days before any permanent local hire was made. The ones that struggled most had a country manager in place from day one with no validated model to operate.

Mistake four: underestimating last-mile operations.

Digital products in Southeast Asia have physical dependencies that products in the US or Europe do not. Payment collection has a cash-on-delivery layer in most markets that requires operational infrastructure. Customer support runs through WhatsApp in Indonesia, LINE in Thailand, and Zalo in Vietnam — not through a ticketing system. Delivery logistics in Philippine archipelagic geography require partners and pricing structures that take months to negotiate properly. Regulatory approvals in Vietnam for fintech products involve government bodies that operate on timelines that cannot be accelerated with urgency or money.

These are not edge cases. They are the default operating environment. A GTM plan that does not account for them is not a plan; it is a series of assumptions that will each become a separate crisis in months two through six of the launch.

The five mistakes, summarised.

  • 01
    Treating SEA as one market. It is ten distinct markets. Country-level specificity is not optional — it is the minimum unit of planning. "Southeast Asia expansion" is not a strategy.
  • 02
    Copying the Singapore playbook. Singapore is an outlier in every metric that matters for consumer and SME products. Build the Indonesia or Thailand playbook in-market, from first principles.
  • 03
    Hiring before proving. A country manager hired before the model is validated becomes a very expensive discovery exercise. Prove the model with a senior operator, then hire to scale it.
  • 04
    Ignoring last-mile operations. Payments, support channels, logistics, and regulatory timelines have physical dependencies in SEA that software-first thinking consistently underestimates.
  • 05
    Running the launch remotely. Every successful market entry we have been part of had a senior operator physically in the market for at least the first thirty days. Dialling in from headquarters produces dialled-in results.

Frequently asked questions.

What are the biggest mistakes when entering Southeast Asia markets?

The five most common are: treating SEA as one homogeneous market, using Singapore as a template for Indonesia, hiring a country manager before proving the model, underestimating last-mile operational dependencies, and running the launch remotely without in-market presence.

Should I enter Singapore or Indonesia first?

Singapore first is legitimate if your product targets enterprise or regional HQ buyers. But if your eventual market is consumer or SME in Southeast Asia, starting in Singapore and copying the playbook to Indonesia will cost you 12–18 months of wasted capital. Better to run a 6-week Indonesia pilot while using Singapore as a base of operations.

How long does a Southeast Asia market entry take?

A properly resourced market entry — first 30 days of operations, first local hire, first paying customers — can be done in 6–8 weeks with an experienced operator embedded in-market. Most founders budget 3 months and take 9 because they're managing it remotely.

Do I need a local partner to enter Indonesia or Thailand?

No legal requirement for most tech products, but a local operator is operationally essential. Regulatory mapping, vendor activation, and the first distribution channel are all faster with someone who has done it before in that specific market — not just "in Asia."
