Fundraising in Asia: what local VCs actually look for

The founder who walks into a Sequoia SEA partner meeting with a slide deck calibrated to Sequoia US expectations will leave without a term sheet and without understanding why. The filters are not the same. The market size math is not the same. The exit narrative is not the same. Learning this on the fifteenth rejection is expensive.

How SEA VC investing differs structurally.

Southeast Asia's venture capital ecosystem is approximately 15 years behind the US in fund maturity. The largest SEA-focused funds — Sequoia Southeast Asia (now Peak XV), Grab Ventures, and GIC's venture arm — are managing $500M–$1B vehicles. Most active SEA-focused funds are significantly smaller: $50M–$200M, run by 3-5 partners, with the operational structure of a tight partnership rather than the institutional machinery of a Andreessen Horowitz or Bessemer. This size difference has downstream effects on everything from diligence speed to portfolio support to LP reporting requirements.

Small fund size means concentrated portfolios. A $100M fund that makes 20-25 investments has no room for portfolio experiments — every investment needs to be defensible to LPs, many of whom are family offices and corporates with shorter time horizons than US pension fund or endowment LPs. This creates a different risk appetite: SEA VCs at seed and Series A are often more conservative on business model validation than their US counterparts, because their LPs are watching every deployment decision more closely.

It also means check sizes are smaller. Seed checks from established SEA VCs run $250K–$1M. Series A checks run $3M–$8M for the leading regional funds. If you have been calibrated on US fundraising expectations where a YC company raises a $3M seed at a $15M cap and a $15M Series A from a top-tier US VC, you will need to recalibrate your expectations significantly. The capital available in the SEA ecosystem is real and growing, but it is not yet at US scale, and the valuation comparables reflect that.

The unit economics filter that most founders fail.

The single most common reason a qualified SEA startup gets passed by SEA VCs is unit economics presented in the wrong context. US growth-stage narratives emphasise topline momentum — GMV, revenue growth rate, user acquisition — and accept high burn rates as the cost of winning fast. This narrative works when the exit environment includes NASDAQ IPOs and US strategic acquirers who pay 15-20x revenue multiples.

In Southeast Asia, the exit environment is different. The region has produced two decacorn exits as of 2026 — Grab (NASDAQ via SPAC, market cap significantly below IPO peak) and GoTo (IDX, similarly challenged post-listing). The realistic exit paths for most SEA startups are: acquisition by a regional conglomerate (Ayala, SM Group, Sinarmas, Central Group, Axiata), acquisition by a global strategic (Delivery Hero buying foodpanda businesses, Prosus acquiring classified platforms), or a Hong Kong or Singapore exchange listing for larger companies. None of these exit paths justify US-style topline-at-all-costs narratives to a sophisticated SEA VC.

What SEA VCs actually weight in unit economics: contribution margin after all variable costs including localised customer success, CAC payback period in local currency accounting for local marketing cost structures, churn rate adjusted for the relationship-sale retention dynamics of the market, and net revenue retention in the customer base. Founders who can show a 14-month CAC payback, 85% gross retention, and positive contribution margin at current scale — even on modest revenue — are more fundable to most SEA VCs than founders showing 300% YoY growth with -40% contribution margins and no clear path to payback.

Founder-market fit: the question behind every question.

The concept of founder-market fit is not new, but in the SEA context it carries a specific regional dimension that US-based investors do not typically probe. A Southeast Asian VC is asking: does this founder have the relationships, the cultural fluency, and the operational knowledge to build a business in this specific geography? Not in "Southeast Asia" as a concept, but in Surabaya or Cebu or Chiang Mai, where the actual customers live and where the operational challenges are encountered.

This is why SEA VCs are often sceptical of Singapore-based founders with no prior operational experience in their target markets pitching Indonesia or Vietnam stories. Singapore is a convenient and tax-efficient base; it is not a proxy for understanding the market. A founder who has spent 18 months in Jakarta, hired 15 people there, navigated the Manpower Act and BPJS Ketenagakerjaan (employment social security), and built 40 customer relationships in the target vertical is signalling a very different founder-market fit than a founder who has visited three times and done desk research.

Insignia Ventures Partners — one of the more intellectually rigorous SEA funds — explicitly asks founders to walk through their operational experience in their target market before discussing the product. Monk's Hill Ventures runs a similar founder deep-dive that is explicitly about whether the team has the operator instincts for the specific geographies they are targeting. East Ventures, the most active early-stage fund in Indonesia, is almost exclusively focused on founders who can speak to the Indonesian market from direct experience — they have backed Ruangguru, Koinworks, and Xendit not because of the products but because of founders who demonstrated Indonesia-specific insight that outsiders could not replicate quickly.

The distribution advantage they want to see.

The question that SEA VCs ask that US VCs rarely do: "What is your distribution advantage in this specific market that a well-funded competitor cannot buy in 12 months?" The US VC version of this question is about technology moat or network effects. The SEA version is about distribution — because in Southeast Asia, the primary competitive advantage is usually not the product (products can be replicated) but the distribution channel.

Distribution advantages that SEA VCs recognise as durable: exclusive channel partnerships with industry associations or distribution networks (e.g., a fintech that has exclusive technology partnerships with three regional bank cooperatives and their combined 200,000 member businesses); a community-led distribution model with genuine organic referral mechanics (e.g., a health platform that has built a community of 50,000 Indonesian users through a combination of Islamic health content and WhatsApp community management that cannot be replicated by purchasing Google Ads); government or quasi-government endorsement in a regulated sector that requires 18 months of relationship-building to obtain; and data network effects specific to the local context (e.g., a logistics platform that has accumulated 3 years of route optimization data for Indonesian tier-3 city delivery that a new entrant cannot replicate without the same time investment).

If your pitch answer to "what is your distribution advantage?" is "our product is better," you are not ready for this meeting. The product being better is table stakes; the distribution advantage is what determines whether a better-resourced competitor can reproduce your traction. Come to the VC meeting with a specific, named distribution advantage and evidence for why it is defensible.

Southeast Asian VCs are not looking for the next Grab. They are looking for the company that builds to $20M ARR in a defensible vertical and is acquired by a regional conglomerate at 6x revenue. The pitch that works is calibrated to that exit, not to a NASDAQ fantasy.

Reframing your pitch without misrepresenting your company.

The reframe from US pitch to SEA pitch is not about changing what your company does — it is about changing what you emphasise and what metrics you foreground. Four specific adjustments make the difference.

First: lead with the market inefficiency, not the market size. US pitches open with TAM — "the HR software market is $15 billion." SEA VCs are sceptical of TAM slides because they know that regional TAM is constrained by ability-to-pay and by the actual distribution channels that exist in the market. Instead, open with the specific operational inefficiency you are solving and evidence of the pain: "Indonesian manufacturing SMEs spend an average of 22 hours per month on manual compliance reporting to BPJS Ketenagakerjaan. We have automated this to 45 minutes. Here are 12 customers who have confirmed this." Specific, documented pain beats TAM abstraction.

Second: present unit economics in local currency context. A CAC of $200 in Indonesia looks very different from a CAC of $200 in the US when average Indonesian SME monthly ARPU is $40 and US ARPU is $200. Show CAC-to-LTV ratios with explicit local currency assumptions. Show your customer acquisition breakdown by channel — direct sales versus referral versus inbound — because SEA VCs know that inbound-driven acquisition in Indonesia is structurally more expensive than relationship-driven acquisition and will probe your channel mix accordingly.

Third: name your exit scenario specifically. "We will IPO" is not a credible SEA exit narrative for most companies below $100M ARR. "We are building the leading HR compliance software for Indonesian manufacturing SMEs with $8M ARR, and our natural acquirer is either Mekari (who has been acquisitive in the HR space) or a global HR software company entering Indonesia (SAP SuccessFactors, Workday, or Darwinbox) who needs the local compliance layer we have built" is a specific, credible narrative that gives the VC a clear path to liquidity.

Fourth: frame your team's operational experience in the market, not just their credentials. SEA VCs care less about whether you went to Stanford than whether you have operated in the specific market you are targeting. Lead with your team's in-market time, customer relationships built, and operational decisions made — then credential. A team that has 3 years of combined Indonesian SME sales experience and 30 direct customer relationships in the manufacturing vertical will be weighted over a team with better credentials and 6 months of in-market presence.

  • 01
    Build your investor target list from portfolio companies, not fund brand names. Find the 10 companies in the most active SEA funds' portfolios that are closest to your vertical and stage. Cold-email the founders of those portfolio companies for introductions. A warm introduction from a portfolio company founder is 8-10x more likely to result in a partner meeting than a cold email to the fund. The portfolio is public; the introduction path takes two weeks of effort.
  • 02
    Lead diligence conversations with customer evidence, not projections. SEA VCs have seen enough hockey-stick projections to be immune to them. What moves the conversation is a 30-minute customer reference call with one of your best users, conducted during the diligence process, where the VC partner can hear directly about the operational pain and the product's impact. Arrange 3-5 reference calls before closing the round, not after the term sheet.
  • 03
    Show path to profitability, not just growth rate. US VCs tolerate negative contribution margins at early stage if growth is sufficiently fast. SEA VCs — particularly those with family office LPs who have shorter horizons — want to see a credible path to contribution-margin breakeven within 18-24 months of the round. This does not mean you have to be profitable now; it means your use-of-funds slide should map to specific unit economics milestones, not just headcount growth.
  • 04
    Name your distribution advantage and defend it specifically. "First mover advantage" is not a distribution moat. "We have a 3-year exclusive technology partnership with APINDO (the Indonesian Employers Association) covering 60,000 member companies" is. Identify your single most defensible distribution advantage and build two slides around it — what it is, why it took time to build, and why a new entrant with $10M cannot replicate it in 18 months.
  • 05
    Calibrate your valuation ask to the SEA exit environment, then negotiate. Anchoring to US comps in a SEA fundraise signals that you are not sophisticated about the local investment context. Research the last 10 Series A rounds in your vertical and stage in SEA (Crunchbase, DealStreetAsia, Tech in Asia). Anchor your pre-money ask to the top quartile of those comps, not to US benchmarks. You can negotiate up; you cannot easily recover from anchoring 3x above what local investors consider reasonable for your traction.

Frequently asked questions.

Which SEA VCs are most active at the seed and Series A stage?

At seed: Iterative (YC-affiliated, strong in SEA), Antler (operator-in-residence model, Singapore-based), Altara Ventures, Openspace Ventures, and DST Partners. For country-specific seed: East Ventures (Indonesia dominant), 1337 Ventures (Malaysia), and Hustle Fund (regional). At Series A: Sequoia Southeast Asia (rebranded as Peak XV), Monk's Hill Ventures, Insignia Ventures Partners, and Alpha JWC Ventures (Indonesia-focused). The landscape changes quickly — check Crunchbase for recent funding data and look for who led the last 5 rounds in your vertical before building your target list.

Do I need to be incorporated in Singapore to raise from SEA VCs?

Not necessarily, but it helps. Most Singapore-based VCs invest comfortably into Singapore holding companies with operational subsidiaries in the target market. Cayman Islands holding structures are also common for companies planning to raise US institutional money later. Indonesian VCs like East Ventures and Alpha JWC will invest into Indonesian PT structures. The practical advice: if you are raising under $2M, incorporate where your operations are and don't over-engineer the structure. Above $2M, get a corporate lawyer familiar with SEA venture structures to advise on holding company setup before you close your round.

How important are warm introductions to SEA VCs?

Extremely important — more so than in the US, where some VCs have invested meaningfully in cold inbound processes. SEA VC partnerships are small (typically 3-5 partners managing $50M–$250M funds) and deal flow is relationship-curated. A warm introduction from a portfolio company founder is the most effective path. Second best: an introduction from a co-investor or angel who has done a previous deal with the target VC. Cold emails convert at under 2% for most SEA VCs. Spend time getting the introduction rather than perfecting the cold email.

What is the typical check size and valuation expectation for a Series A in Southeast Asia?

Series A in SEA typically ranges from $3M–$12M at pre-money valuations of $10M–$40M, compared to US Series A norms of $10M–$20M at $30M–$80M pre-money. The compression is real and it is a function of market size expectations and exit comparables. Indonesian and Philippine founders often push back on these valuations by citing US comparables, which frustrates SEA investors who are pricing to the regional exit environment. The better approach: accept the SEA valuation premium at seed and Series A, build regional revenue proof, and attract US or global institutional money at Series B where the market size argument becomes credible.
