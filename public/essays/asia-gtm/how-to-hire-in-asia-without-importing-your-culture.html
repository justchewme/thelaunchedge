How to hire in Asia without importing your culture

The most consistent hiring mistake Western companies make in Asia is optimising for cultural fit to headquarters rather than operational effectiveness in market. The result is a team that communicates fluently upward, performs beautifully in Zoom calls with the executive team, and moves glacially in the actual market it was hired to compete in.

The ambassador problem.

When a Western company hires for a leadership role in Singapore, Jakarta, or Ho Chi Minh City, the interview process is almost always conducted in English, by a hiring committee that includes at least one or two people from the company's Western headquarters, using a competency framework developed for the parent company's culture. The candidates who perform best in this process are, by definition, the candidates who are most skilled at performing well in a Western corporate context. They are articulate in English, comfortable with the communication norms of American or European business culture, fluent in the vocabulary of whatever management framework the company uses, and able to mirror the values and expectations of the interviewers across the table.

The trouble is that none of these qualities are what makes a leader effective in an Asian market. What makes a leader effective in Jakarta is knowledge of the distribution networks, relationships with the key distributors and community connectors, understanding of how decisions are made in Indonesian organisations, and the ability to recruit and retain talent in a competitive local market. What makes a leader effective in Ho Chi Minh City is the ability to navigate the regulatory environment, build trust with local enterprise customers who buy based on relationship rather than product features, and manage a team whose communication norms are different from a Western flat-culture startup. These competencies do not show up in an English-language interview conducted by Western executives. So they are rarely selected for.

What gets selected is what I call the ambassador profile: the candidate who can communicate the company to the market and the market to the company, but who is fundamentally oriented toward the internal relationship with headquarters rather than the external challenge of winning in market. Ambassadors are excellent at status updates, quarterly business reviews, and ensuring that headquarters feels informed and comfortable. They are poor at the unglamorous work of building distribution from scratch, hiring and developing local teams, and making the market adaptations that headquarters would prefer not to know about because they require deviating from the global playbook.

What operators look like, and why they are harder to hire.

The operator profile is almost the opposite of the ambassador. Operators are often less polished in English, more opinionated about the local market in ways that make Western executives uncomfortable, and less interested in internal communication than in external execution. They will tell you that your product pricing is wrong for the market in a way that sounds like criticism of headquarters' decisions rather than market intelligence. They will push for adaptations that create complexity for the global product team. They will spend more time in the field, with customers and distributors, than in the office preparing materials for executive review.

These qualities make operators worse interview candidates by the metrics most companies use, while making them dramatically better at the actual job. The irony is that the hiring process filters out exactly the people who could succeed.

There are specific markers to look for. An operator in the Indonesian market will be able to tell you, without consulting any document, the names of the five largest distributors in their sector, where they are based, and what their typical terms are. They will have an opinion on which distributor is most reliable and which is most aggressive in cross-selling to competitors. They will know which regency governments are currently hostile to their product category and why. They will have a mental model of the competitive landscape that includes not just the obvious international competitors but the local players that most foreign observers have never heard of. This knowledge does not come from research. It comes from being in the market, every day, for years.

The interview test I recommend: ask your final candidates to walk you through the last three decisions they made that cost them political capital internally. Operators have clear answers here — they have had real conflicts with headquarters over resource allocation, product adaptation, or market strategy, and they made calls they believed were right despite the friction. Ambassadors struggle with this question because their career success has depended on minimising internal conflict rather than resolving market problems. The friction an operator describes is the evidence that they were doing real work.

Singapore as a hiring trap.

Singapore is where many Western companies establish their Asia regional headquarters, and therefore where they often make their first Asian hires. The Singapore talent market is highly educated, English-fluent, experienced with multinational corporate environments, and geographically convenient for executives flying in from London or New York. It is also a market that systematically produces ambassador profiles rather than operator profiles, for structural reasons that are worth understanding.

Singapore's economic model is built on being a regional hub for multinational corporations. A significant portion of Singapore's professional talent pool has built careers inside MNC regional offices — doing regional coordination, strategy work, and upward reporting for parent companies that run operations in more complex neighbouring markets. These are genuinely useful skills in a regional headquarters context. They are not the skills you need to build market share in Surabaya or Cebu.

When a Western company hires a "Southeast Asia Head" or "Indonesia Country Manager" from the Singapore talent pool, they often get a candidate who has worked at three or four regional MNC offices, has an MBA from a respected institution, and can run a market analysis slide deck with genuine competence. What they often do not get is a candidate who has spent three years building a sales team in East Java, navigating the bureaucracy of a provincial government regulatory approval, or growing a distribution network in a Tier 3 city where English is not spoken and WhatsApp groups are the primary communication infrastructure. That candidate is in Indonesia, not Singapore.

You are not hiring for the organisational capability to manage a relationship with your headquarters. You are hiring for the market capability to win against local competitors who know things about your customers that you do not.

The hierarchy question that Western companies get wrong.

One of the most common complaints Western founders make about their Asian teams is that employees do not push back, do not voice concerns in group settings, and wait for direction rather than taking initiative. The conclusion drawn from this observation is usually that the team needs training in "flat culture" or "psychological safety" or "ownership mindset." This diagnosis is almost always wrong, and the attempted remedy — importing Western culture through culture decks and workshops — almost always makes things worse.

What is actually happening is a communication style difference that is being misread as a performance or motivation problem. In Indonesian, Vietnamese, and many other Asian organisational contexts, it is considered socially inappropriate to challenge a superior's position in a group setting. This is not passivity. It is a sophisticated social protocol that prioritises group harmony and protects the face of all participants. Employees in these contexts have opinions. They express those opinions through specific channels — one-on-one conversations with trusted superiors, pre-meeting informal discussions, or through a trusted intermediary who can raise the concern without public confrontation. The Western expectation that dissent should be voiced in the meeting room is not a norm that employees have failed to learn. It is a norm that is in conflict with deeply-held social values, and you will not override it with a culture document.

The practical solution is to redesign your information-gathering processes rather than trying to change your team's communication style. Create explicit one-on-one channels for feedback. Ask specific questions in private that you would not expect answers to in public. Develop a relationship with one or two trusted senior local team members who function as cultural translators — not in the linguistic sense, but in the organisational sense — who can surface concerns to you that the broader team would never express in a group setting. This is not a workaround. It is how effective organisations are run in these contexts.

Structuring the hiring process to find operators.

The changes required to hire operators rather than ambassadors are mostly changes to what you look for and how you evaluate it, not changes to where you look. In practice, the best operators are usually not on LinkedIn with polished profiles. They are running businesses or teams inside existing organisations, and they will not apply for a role — they need to be recruited through industry networks, former colleagues who know them, or sector-specific headhunters who have real market relationships rather than database access.

For a country manager role in Indonesia, the search should start by talking to ten to fifteen people who are active in the relevant sector — investors, other operators, customers, suppliers — and asking them who the best operators they have encountered in the last three years are. The same names will recur. Those people are your shortlist, regardless of whether they are currently looking for a new role. This is not a passive recruitment process. It requires someone with enough market credibility to make those calls and enough relationship capital to get honest answers.

The interview process itself should be reweighted away from English communication fluency and toward demonstrated market knowledge and operational track record. Require candidates to do a 30-minute market briefing on the competitive landscape in your sector, delivered as if they were briefing a new colleague, not an executive. Evaluate the quality of the market knowledge rather than the quality of the presentation. Ask for specific examples of decisions made under resource constraints, failures that produced learning, and adaptations made to strategy when the original plan did not work. These questions reveal operators. They reveal very little about ambassadors.

The salary reality check.

Western companies often arrive in Asia with salary expectations calibrated to either (a) their home market, which makes them uncompetitive for senior talent in Singapore, or (b) a romanticised notion of Asian salary levels that severely undervalues the operators they need to hire. Both errors are expensive in different ways.

In Singapore, a country manager or regional head with genuine operating experience — the person who can actually build your business rather than manage the reporting — commands SGD 180,000–300,000 per year in base salary, plus bonus and equity. Trying to hire this person at SGD 100,000 will get you either someone who is not as experienced as they appear, or someone who is using your role as a bridge to something better and will leave within 18 months. In Indonesia, the operator profile you need at country director level — someone with local market relationships, distribution knowledge, and the ability to build a team — will expect IDR 300–600 million annually from a well-funded startup, and potentially USD 50,000–80,000 if they have prior international company experience and know their market value. Offering IDR 150 million for this role produces exactly the result you should expect: you get someone for whom this is a step up, not a hire that is a step up for you.

  • 01
    Rewrite your job description to specify operator capabilities, not ambassador capabilities. "Excellent communication skills" selects for ambassadors. "Track record of building distribution in Tier 2 and 3 Indonesian cities" selects for operators. The language of the job description signals what you value, which determines who applies.
  • 02
    Start the search with a market network, not a LinkedIn job post. The operator you need is almost certainly not on the job market. They need to be recruited through industry relationships. Hire a headhunter with real sector relationships in the target market — not a generalist Singapore-based recruiter — or invest six to eight weeks of your own time building the network to find the right person.
  • 03
    Use the competitor knowledge test in every final round interview. Ask your candidate to brief you on the competitive landscape as if you are a new colleague. Operators give you names, relationships, price points, channel strategies, and opinions. Ambassadors give you a SWOT framework with publicly available information you could have Googled.
  • 04
    Do not require flat-culture fluency as a hiring criterion. Operators who have built businesses in hierarchical cultural contexts may communicate very differently from what you expect. Do not mistake communication style for motivation or capability. Evaluate what people have built, not how they talk about building it.
  • 05
    Budget for market-rate salaries from the start. Underpricing your first Asia hire — the country manager or market lead whose decisions will determine whether your expansion works — is a false economy. The cost of a bad hire in this role, measured in 18 months of lost market development time, is far higher than the premium required to hire the right person.
  • 06
    Build explicit feedback channels that match local communication norms. Do not expect your team to challenge decisions publicly. Create the private channels — weekly one-on-ones, anonymous feedback mechanisms, trusted intermediary relationships — that are appropriate to how feedback is actually communicated in the local context. Then listen to what comes through those channels.

Frequently asked questions.

Should my first hire in a new Asian market be a local or an expat?

Almost always a local, with one important exception. If the role is specifically about translating between your HQ and the local market — interpreting market intelligence for a Western leadership team, building the internal case for local adaptations — an experienced returnee (someone locally educated who has worked in the West and returned) can bridge both worlds. For any role that requires building market relationships, managing local teams, or navigating regulatory environments, a local operator with relevant market experience will consistently outperform an expatriate, at lower cost and with better retention. The expat who insists on a Singapore cost-of-living package to run your Jakarta operations is also extracting a significant premium for the market access you need, which compounds over time.

How do I evaluate whether a candidate has genuine market knowledge vs. interview polish?

Ask them to describe three decisions a competitor made in the past 12 months that surprised them, and explain what they think drove those decisions. Good operators in the market will have specific answers, often with opinions. Ambassadors — people who present well but operate from surface-level market familiarity — will give you a general summary that sounds like a market research report. The other test: ask them to describe a customer they lost and why. Operators have post-mortems. Ambassadors have frameworks. A third test: ask what they would change about your product for this market, specifically. Operators have a list. Ambassadors compliment your product and offer to learn more.

How should I handle the hierarchy expectations that many Asian employees bring to the workplace?

Stop trying to override them with culture. Hierarchical communication patterns in many Asian workplaces are not dysfunction — they are a different and internally consistent approach to decision-making that reflects cultural values around respect, face, and consensus. The most effective approach is to make your decision-making process explicit rather than expecting employees to intuit a flat structure. If you want people to challenge decisions, tell them when and how — and create a specific private channel for that challenge, because a public challenge in front of peers is not something many employees will be comfortable with regardless of what your culture deck says. Importing the assumption of flat culture produces teams that are confused about what is expected of them, not teams that are empowered.

What salary ranges should I expect for senior hires in Singapore, Indonesia, and Vietnam?

Singapore: a country manager or VP-level hire with 10+ years experience typically commands SGD 180,000–300,000 base, plus bonus and equity. A strong senior manager is SGD 90,000–140,000. Indonesia: a country director at a well-funded startup runs IDR 300–600 million per year (roughly USD 18,000–37,000) for local hiring; candidates with prior MNC or funded startup experience may ask for USD 50,000–80,000 and are often worth it. Vietnam: senior commercial leads in HCMC typically earn USD 2,500–5,000 per month; engineering talent is USD 2,000–4,000 per month at senior level. These ranges shift quickly in competitive talent markets — verify with a local recruiter before publishing a job description with a salary range.
