How to close enterprise deals in Asia

Enterprise sales in Asia does not follow the champion-driven, demo-to-close sequence that US sales methodology was built around. The trust-building phases are longer, the buying committee is wider, procurement decisions run through relationship networks rather than formal RFPs, and payment terms reflect cash flow norms that are market-specific and non-negotiable. The enterprise sales process that closes in Singapore, Indonesia, and the Philippines is not the same process — and applying one to another without adjustment will cost you deals.

The consensus buying model and why champions are not enough.

US enterprise sales methodology places the champion at the centre of the deal architecture. Find the internal buyer who needs your product, build their conviction, arm them with business case materials, help them navigate internal procurement, and close through them. This model works in organisations where individual decision-making authority is clearly scoped and where a single champion with budget authority can move a purchasing decision through procurement without needing group consensus at each stage.

Asian enterprise organisations are not structured this way. In Indonesian conglomerates, Thai family-controlled businesses, and Philippine government-linked companies, purchasing decisions are made through a consensus process where the legitimacy of the decision depends on the buy-in of multiple stakeholders — not because each stakeholder has formal veto power, but because the social cost of being the person who pushed through a decision that others opposed is high enough that champions avoid doing it. The consequence for your sales process is that you are not selling to a champion. You are building a coalition inside the account, with your champion as the lead organiser of that coalition.

The practical implication is that your sales process needs deliberate multi-threading — active relationships with multiple people in the account from early in the cycle. Not just the champion, but their manager (who needs to see the ROI case), the IT or technical evaluator (who needs to see the security and integration story), and the end users who will actually use the product (whose adoption rate will determine whether the purchase was justified). In Indonesian enterprise accounts, it is not uncommon for a product champion to push a deal through the budget approval process and then have it stall at the IT security review, where a technical evaluator who has never been engaged in the sales process raises objections that the champion is not equipped to answer.

The trust-building phase cannot be compressed.

The most consistent mistake that US-trained enterprise sellers make in Asia is treating the trust-building phase as a delay to be managed rather than work to be done. In a US enterprise sale, the trust-building phase is compressed into a polished deck, a well-run demo, credible customer references, and a professional company website. In Asia, the trust-building phase requires time and physical presence — it cannot be substituted by materials, no matter how good they are.

In Indonesia, a first enterprise meeting is rarely the beginning of a commercial negotiation. It is the beginning of a getting-to-know-you process that will take multiple meetings over several weeks before the buyer is comfortable enough to table a commercial discussion. The buyer is assessing whether you are someone they can work with over a multi-year relationship — not just whether your product solves their problem. They are asking: Does this company understand Indonesia? Will this vendor still be serving us in three years? Is the person I am talking to going to be my account manager or a sales rep who disappears after the contract is signed?

The fastest way to compress the trust-building phase legitimately is local reference customers. An Indonesian enterprise buyer who knows that your product is already being used by a respected Indonesian company — particularly a company in their network, a competitor they respect, or a company associated with their own conglomerate group — has their trust question substantially answered. The first meeting becomes much more commercial much faster when the account can triangulate your credibility through an existing relationship rather than building it from scratch.

This is why land-and-expand is the dominant enterprise sales motion that works in Asia. The first customer in a market is the most expensive acquisition in terms of time invested. The second customer comes faster because you have the first as a reference. By the fifth customer, you have enough of a local reference network that the trust-building phase compresses to 2-4 weeks rather than 3-6 months. Invest disproportionately in closing the first customer in each new market, even if the deal economics do not make sense in isolation.

Procurement in Asia: what formal and informal mean.

The formal procurement process in Asian enterprise companies — the RFP, the vendor evaluation committee, the procurement checklist — exists and is real. But in most private and family-controlled Asian enterprises, the formal procurement process validates a decision that was already made informally, rather than being the mechanism through which the decision is made.

The practical consequence is that vendors who appear in an RFP for the first time — without prior relationship with any stakeholder in the account — are almost always filling a compliance requirement. The buyer needs at least three quotes to satisfy their procurement policy. Your quote is the third quote that makes the process compliant. This is worth knowing so that you do not invest significant pre-sales resources into an RFP where you have no prior relationship and no champion. The right response to an inbound RFP where you have no relationship is to use it as an entry point to build a relationship for the next cycle, not to compete for a deal that is already decided.

The informal procurement process — the dinner conversation where the CPO of a major Indonesian bank mentions to a mutual contact that they are evaluating treasury management tools, the WhatsApp group discussion in an industry association where a CHRO mentions their HR system renewal is coming up — is where the real procurement decisions begin. Being present in these networks, through community participation, customer referrals, and event attendance, is how you surface opportunities before they become formal RFPs. By the time an RFP is published, the informal decision is usually largely made.

In Asian enterprise sales, the deal is won or lost in the six months before the contract is drafted. The contract negotiation is a formality. The trust-building is the work.

Payment terms and what they actually mean for cash flow.

Payment terms in Asian enterprise deals differ significantly from US norms, and founders building financial models without accounting for this regularly face cash flow surprises in their first year of market operations.

In Indonesia, net-60 to net-90 is the standard expectation from enterprise customers — particularly from state-owned enterprises (BUMN) and government-linked companies, where disbursement processes follow government fiscal cycles. A BUMN customer may not process payment until its next budget disbursement window, regardless of what the contract payment terms specify. For SaaS companies that model their cash flow on monthly or quarterly billings, a single large BUMN deal can create a multi-month cash collection lag that complicates burn rate calculations.

The mitigation for long payment terms is annual upfront billing — a common structure in Singapore enterprise SaaS sales, where customers pay for the full year at contract signing. This is achievable in Indonesian and Philippine enterprise markets for deals where your champion has budget authority and is not constrained by procurement-controlled disbursement. The negotiating lever is a discount for annual upfront payment — typically 10-15% off the annual contract value, which for a $50,000 ACV deal represents $5,000-$7,500 and is usually worth the cash flow benefit of receiving the full $42,500-$45,000 at signing rather than twelve monthly installments of uncertain timing.

In Singapore, payment terms are more standardised — net-30 is common for mid-market accounts and net-45 to net-60 for large enterprise. Singapore corporates generally pay on time, and the legal enforcement of contract terms is more reliable. For companies building a regional enterprise book, the Singapore revenue tends to have better cash flow characteristics than the Indonesia or Philippines books, which is worth factoring into growth planning and fundraise timing.

Market-specific differences: Singapore, Indonesia, and the Philippines.

Singapore enterprise sales is the closest SEA market to Western enterprise sales in its mechanics. Champions have more individual decision-making authority. Procurement processes are more formalised and transparent. Security and compliance reviews follow documented frameworks (typically aligned to MAS TRM guidelines for financial services). Deal timelines are more predictable. English-language communication is universal. The sales rep from a US company who excels in Singapore may struggle in Indonesia or the Philippines not because they are less skilled, but because the mechanics are genuinely different.

Indonesian enterprise sales requires the consensus-building, trust-investment approach described above, with the additional complexity of navigating Bahasa Indonesia communication preferences in internal stakeholder meetings, hierarchy sensitivities that affect how information flows up to decision-makers, and the procurement influence of the IT department (which is powerful in Indonesian enterprises and often has an informal vendor list that is difficult to break into without an existing relationship at the technical level).

Philippine enterprise sales has characteristics of both. Conglomerate accounts (Ayala, SM, JG Summit, Metro Pacific) operate more like Indonesian accounts — consensus-driven, relationship-heavy, long trust-building phases. Technology company accounts and the subsidiaries of multinational corporations in BGC operate more like Singapore accounts — more individual authority, faster procurement, clearer decision rights. The Philippine government sector is its own category: procurement is governed by the New Government Procurement Act (RA 12009, which replaced RA 9184 in 2024), which mandates formal bidding processes that must be navigated with specific technical and legal expertise. Direct sales to Philippine government accounts without a local government business development specialist is not advisable for a foreign company entering the market for the first time.

  • 01
    Multi-thread every account from the first meeting. Identify the champion, their manager, the technical evaluator, and the end-user representative in the first three meetings. Build direct relationships with each. A single-threaded account is one executive departure away from starting over. In markets with high executive mobility — Philippines tech sector, Indonesian startup ecosystem — single-threaded enterprise deals have a failure rate that should be treated as a structural risk, not an exceptional event.
  • 02
    Invest in your first local reference customer at below-market economics. The first Indonesian, Thai, or Philippine enterprise customer is not a commercial deal — it is a distribution asset. Price it to close, not to maximise. A pilot at 50% of your standard rate that converts to a full contract and becomes a named reference is worth more than a lost deal at standard pricing. The second and third customers will close faster and at better terms because the first one exists. Model the cost of the first customer as customer acquisition cost, not as revenue.
  • 03
    Surface the full buying committee before your demo, not during it. Ask your champion in the first meeting: who else will be involved in this decision? The typical answer in an Asian enterprise account will reveal a list of 3-7 people. Request separate introductory meetings with each person before running a formal demo for the group. A group demo where half the room has not been pre-briefed on your company and your product produces questions that derail the commercial progression. Individual pre-briefings mean the group demo is a consolidation of already-formed positive impressions.
  • 04
    Structure payment terms into the deal before the commercial proposal. Before you submit a proposal with standard payment terms, have a direct conversation with your champion about what payment terms their procurement process can accommodate. The answer will tell you whether to structure annual upfront with a discount (for champions with budget authority), quarterly billing (for accounts with Q-cycle budget releases), or the 60-90 day invoice terms that procurement-controlled accounts require. Surprises in the payment terms negotiation are avoidable if you surface the procurement reality before the proposal is sent.
  • 05
    Use pilots strategically, not as a discounting mechanism. Pilots in Asian enterprise sales are often used by buyers as a delay tactic — a way to evaluate without committing. Structure your pilot with specific success criteria defined before it begins, a time limit (30-60 days maximum), and a commercial term sheet agreed alongside the pilot scope that converts automatically to a full contract if the criteria are met. A pilot without a pre-agreed conversion path is a free trial dressed in enterprise language.
  • 06
    Show up to the relationship events your buyers attend. The industry dinners, the association AGMs, the government-hosted B2B forums, the golf tournaments that are really pipeline events in disguise. In Asian enterprise sales, being present in the same social and professional ecosystem as your buyers creates the informal familiarity that precedes formal purchasing conversations. An enterprise buyer in Jakarta who has seen your CEO three times at Indonesia Fintech Summit has a different baseline of trust for your company than one who is encountering you through a cold email sequence. Budget for presence. It is not entertainment — it is distribution infrastructure.

Frequently asked questions.

How long is a typical enterprise sales cycle in Asia?

For mid-market deals ($20,000-$100,000 ACV) in Indonesia: 6-12 months. For enterprise deals ($100,000+ ACV) in Indonesia: 12-24 months. Singapore moves faster — 3-6 months for mid-market, 6-12 months for enterprise — because procurement processes are more formalised and champion authority is more clearly defined. The pattern across all markets is that cycles compressed by founder pressure result in deals that fall through at the last stage, because the internal consensus that was not built cannot be substituted by urgency.

Should I use a formal RFP process or relationship-based selling in Asia?

RFP processes exist in Asia, particularly for government-linked companies and state-owned enterprises. But most mid-market and private enterprise deals do not begin with an RFP — they begin with a relationship. The RFP, when it appears, is typically a procurement formality that validates a decision already made informally. Companies that appear in an RFP for the first time, without a prior relationship, are filling a compliance requirement — not competing for the business.

What payment terms should I expect from Asian enterprise customers?

Indonesian and Philippine enterprise customers commonly request 60-90 day payment terms. State-owned enterprises in Indonesia sometimes run 120-180 day payment cycles due to government budget disbursement processes. For SaaS products, annual upfront billing with a 30-45 day payment term is achievable for mid-market accounts where your champion has budget authority. Build net-60 into your financial model as the standard for enterprise accounts and treat anything faster as a positive outcome.

How do I handle the situation where my champion leaves during a long sales cycle?

This is the most common risk in long-cycle Asian enterprise sales, and the mitigation is multi-threading before your champion leaves, not after. In every account where you are 3+ months into a cycle, you should have relationships with at least three stakeholders at different levels. When the champion leaves, you have a warm entry point to whoever picks up the brief. A single-threaded relationship in a 12-month sales cycle has a much lower chance of surviving to close, because executive turnover in Asian companies runs high.
