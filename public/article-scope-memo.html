The scope memo is the product.

The deck was always a performance. A 60-slide artefact produced to justify the fee that paid for it. Somewhere in the last eighteen months the practice quietly stopped delivering them — and the work got faster, the clients got closer, and the memos got sharper.

The deck is theatre.

Consulting decks are a genre. They have tropes — the appendix of sources nobody will read, the executive summary that repeats itself three times in slightly different fonts, the "implementation roadmap" that is really a Gantt chart of meetings. The deck exists because the deck is the only tangible artefact that justifies the invoice. Nobody rereads it after week three. It sits on a SharePoint until someone's laptop is replaced.

For a while we delivered them too, because the market expected them. Clients would ask, unprompted, "will we get the final deck?" — as though the deck, not the shipped work, was the point.

The switch.

The switch was not dramatic. A founder in Jakarta said, halfway through a scoping call, "don't send me the deck. Send me the contract. Two pages. I'll read it before dinner." We wrote it that night — outcomes, timeline, a fixed quote, what we would not do — and sent it. He signed it the next morning. The engagement was the fastest one we ran that year.

The deck was always a performance. The memo is the contract. Once we stopped performing, the work got faster and the clients got closer.

Anatomy of a scope memo.

Every scope memo we write now has the same shape. It is two pages. It fits in an email. A founder can read it in the time it takes to drink an espresso. Here is the spine:

  • 01
    Outcome. One sentence. Not a goal — a measurable result. "Activation lifted from 22% to 40% on new cohorts within 10 weeks." If we can't commit to that, we don't have the memo yet.
  • 02
    Timeline. Calendar dates, not weeks. "Start: 12 May. Memo review: 26 May. Halfway check: 16 June. Close-out: 14 July." Weeks drift. Dates don't.
  • 03
    Inputs. What the client provides: access, data, a named decision-maker, and the first hour of their Monday. If they can't provide these, the engagement is a fantasy.
  • 04
    Outputs. What is shipped, in the client's system of record. A live funnel. A hired operator. An agent running on their VPS. Not a document.
  • 05
    Price. Flat fee. Paid in two tranches. No hourly billing, no retainer escalator, no "change orders". If the work is bigger than we thought, that's our cost to eat.
  • 06
    What we will not do. The most important section. "We will not write a board deck. We will not run the all-hands. We will not represent the company externally." Explicit boundaries prevent the engagement from shape-shifting.

Why it works.

The memo is not a lighter version of the deck. It is a different instrument. A deck is a performance of expertise; the memo is an act of commitment. A deck is asymmetric — you present, they receive. The memo is symmetric — both sides sign the same two pages. When the memo is the product, the deliverable is decisions, not documents.

There is a second-order effect. Because the memo is short, it has to be true. You cannot hide behind a 60-slide architecture when the whole argument fits on two pages. If you can't justify the outcome, timeline, and price in three paragraphs, you haven't scoped the work yet.

What we lost, what we gained.

We lost the theatre. The handover meeting with twenty people in it. The closing presentation. The sense of ceremony around a pricey engagement. Some senior clients missed it at first. Most did not.

We gained speed. Memos are written in a day, not a fortnight. Engagements start on week one, not week four. Scope is unambiguous. Clients stop asking for "the final deck" because there is nothing to ask for — the final artefact is the thing we built, running in production.

How to try this.

If you are on the client side of a consulting relationship: next time someone sends you a deck, ask them for two pages. Outcomes, timeline, a fixed quote, what they will not do. Most consultancies cannot write it. The ones who can are the ones worth hiring.

If you are on the vendor side: try replacing your next proposal with a memo. It is a strange feeling — fewer slides, smaller Word file, higher close rate. You will miss the theatre for about three weeks, and then you will stop missing it entirely.
